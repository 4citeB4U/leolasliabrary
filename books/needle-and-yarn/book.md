# Dedication

To my children, grandchildren, and great-grandchildren, who have been the threads that bind my life together.

To every hand that has ever held a crochet hook, seeking to create something beautiful.

And especially to those who have yet to discover the joy of creating with yarn and needle — may this story inspire your journey.

With love and faith,

— Sister Lee

# Foreword

In my many years of crocheting, I've come to understand that every stitch tells a story. Just as we loop, twist, and connect yarn to create something beautiful, our lives are a series of connections, breaks, and mending.

This book was born from the quiet hours spent with my hook and yarn, contemplating the lessons learned through this craft that has been my companion through joy and sorrow alike.

To crochet is to understand patience, to recognize that sometimes we must undo our work to create something stronger. It teaches us that mistakes are not failures but opportunities for growth. Most importantly, it reminds us that we are all connected—stitched together in a pattern larger than ourselves.

With love,  
Leola (Sister) Lee  
Milwaukee, Wisconsin  
Spring 2025

# Chapter One — The Magic Begins

The little bell over the shop door gave a bright *ding-a-ling*.

Morning sunshine slipped in right behind a woman and stretched itself long across the old wooden floor.

She didn't come rushing in.

No, ma'am.

She took her time.

There were some places in this world you hurried through, and some places you simply did not.

A good yarn shop was one of those places.

Her shoes moved softly between shelves piled high with every color a body could imagine. There were blues deep as midnight and blues pale as morning sky. Reds bright enough to make you smile just looking at them. Greens like fresh-cut grass. Soft creams and warm browns. Purples that looked downright proud of themselves.

And yarn.

Lord, there was yarn everywhere.

Thick yarn.

Fine yarn.

Wool and cotton.

Big round balls, neat little cakes, twisted hanks and soft skeins stacked clear up toward the ceiling.

The woman's hand moved slowly across them.

Feel.

Squeeze.

Consider.

Move along.

Down near the bottom of a wicker basket sat one particularly bright skein of sunset-orange cotton.

And that little skein had been watching that hand come closer and closer.

“Oh, my stars,” she whispered.

The hand reached toward the basket.

“Come on now. Just a little farther.”

It hovered above her.

Yarn held herself as still as a skein possibly could.

The fingers moved down—

and picked up the cream-colored skein sitting beside her.

Yarn's whole middle sagged.

“Well, ain't that just my luck.”

The cream skein disappeared.

Yarn sat there trying her best not to look disappointed.

“I didn't want to go nowhere anyhow,” she muttered.

The hand returned.

Yarn straightened.

“Now hold on.”

Two fingers pressed gently into her fibers.

“Oh!”

Up she went.

“Good gracious!”

For the first time in her whole life, Yarn could see over the side of that basket.

And mercy, what a world.

Shelves and shelves stretched around her. Little wooden drawers held buttons of every shape. Crochet hooks shined underneath the lights. Needles stood lined up straight and proper like they had someplace important to be.

Yarn turned as much as the hand would allow.

“Well, look at all this.”

The hand turned her over.

A thumb pressed against the paper band wrapped around her middle.

Yarn froze.

She knew about this.

Every skein did.

This was **The Choosing**.

Nobody called it that when customers were around, naturally.

But when the shop grew quiet at night, the yarn talked.

Oh, they had stories.

Some said that when you got chosen, you became scarves.

Others swore you became blankets.

A fuzzy yellow skein once claimed her cousin had become a baby sweater.

And there was this big purple ball on the top shelf who told anybody willing to listen that beyond those doors was a whole world where yarn became hats, dolls, dresses, blankets, sweaters and all manner of beautiful things.

Yarn had always figured Purple was full of foolishness.

Standing there in that woman's hand, she wasn't quite so certain anymore.

The hand placed her gently inside a shopping basket.

Yarn bounced against the bottom.

Once.

Twice.

Then settled.

She whispered the words just to hear herself say them.

“I'm gettin' chosen.”

Her excitement lasted approximately three seconds.

Then another thought came.

“Oh, Lord.”

She had never considered what happened **between** being chosen and becoming something.

She peeped over the basket.

“Where we goin'?”

The woman, of course, didn't answer.

Yarn swallowed.

“Well... wherever it is, I reckon I'm goin'.”

And for reasons she couldn't explain, that made her smile.

---

The journey was a whole lot noisier than Yarn expected.

Doors opened.

Doors closed.

People talked.

Something rumbled underneath her for what felt like forever.

Every now and again, light slipped through the top of the bag.

Yarn tried to see out.

Couldn't.

Tried again.

Still couldn't.

“Well, this ain't helpful.”

Then everything stopped.

Footsteps.

Another door.

And suddenly—

quiet.

Not empty quiet.

Home quiet.

The kind with a clock ticking somewhere.

The faint clink of something being set on a table.

A window cracked just enough to let a little breeze inside.

The bag opened.

Warm light poured down.

Yarn looked up.

“Ohhh.”

The hand reached in and lifted her.

This place was nothing like the shop.

It was smaller.

Warmer.

Lived in.

A wooden table sat near a window. Beside it was a comfortable chair with a cushion worn lower on one side, like somebody had been sitting there for years.

There were books stacked nearby.

Plants reaching toward the light.

A cup rested on a little saucer.

And right beside that chair sat an old wicker basket.

Yarn stared at it.

Now *that* basket had seen some things.

Its sides were worn smooth. Tiny pieces of thread hid between the reeds. A blue stitch marker clung to one corner like it had claimed the place and wasn't leaving for nobody.

Inside were scissors, hooks, scraps of yarn, little bits of unfinished work and tools Yarn couldn't even name.

This wasn't just a basket.

This was where things got made.

The hand lowered her toward it.

“Well,” Yarn whispered, “I reckon this is home.”

She landed softly.

Something underneath a folded piece of fabric moved.

Yarn stopped.

The fabric shifted.

A long silver needle eased himself into view.

He wasn't shiny-new.

No.

Tiny marks traveled along his surface.

Not damage exactly.

History.

The kind a thing collected from being useful for a long, long time.

He looked at Yarn.

Yarn looked at him.

Needle had seen new yarn come into this basket before.

Plenty.

Most of them had enough sense to settle themselves down and wait until they were needed.

This one had been in the basket less than ten seconds and had already inspected the scissors, the window, three stitch markers, an old measuring tape—

and him.

Especially him.

Yarn smiled.

“Well, hey there.”

Needle gave her a polite nod.

“Afternoon.”

Yarn waited.

Needle said nothing else.

She waited some more.

Still nothing.

She rolled a little closer.

“My name's Yarn.”

“So I gathered.”

Yarn blinked.

“Well, ain't you friendly.”

Needle's eye narrowed ever so slightly.

“I said good afternoon.”

“Yes, you surely did. Put your whole heart into it, too.”

Needle looked away.

Yarn rolled closer again.

“So what's your name?”

He looked back at her.

“Needle.”

Yarn stared.

“Needle?”

“Yes.”

She looked at herself.

Then at him.

Then back at herself.

A little giggle escaped.

Needle straightened.

“What?”

“I'm Yarn.”

“I am aware.”

“And you Needle.”

“Yes.”

“And somebody done put Needle and Yarn together in the same basket.”

Needle stared at her.

Yarn's giggle became a laugh.

“Now if that ain't somethin'.”

“There is nothing particularly unusual about—”

“Oh, hush. It's funny.”

“I don't see why.”

“That might be because you look like you ain't laughed since they invented thread.”

Needle tried not to smile.

Yarn caught it anyway.

“Ooooh, there it is.”

“There is what?”

“That smile you tryin' not to have.”

“I am doing no such thing.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

Needle turned away.

Yarn smiled to herself.

She had been in this house five minutes.

She already liked it here.

---

A shadow passed over the basket.

Both of them became still.

The hand.

It moved down among the tools with the confidence of somebody who didn't need to look for anything.

Those fingers knew this basket.

They brushed past a hook.

Moved a scrap aside.

And closed around Needle.

He rose.

Yarn's smile disappeared.

“Wait. Where you goin'?”

Needle looked down toward her.

“I expect we're about to find out.”

Then he disappeared over the basket's edge.

Yarn sat alone.

“Well, that ain't mysterious at all.”

The hand came back.

For her.

Suddenly all that excitement she'd had in the shop went somewhere else.

“Oh.”

The fingers lifted her.

And right then Yarn understood something.

Being chosen wasn't the end of anything.

It was the beginning.

You didn't get chosen just so somebody could admire you.

You got chosen to **become** something.

The hand placed Yarn beside Needle on the wooden table.

In the open afternoon light, they got their first good look at one another.

Needle glanced over.

“Nervous?”

“No.”

He waited.

Yarn sighed.

“Maybe a little.”

“That's all right.”

She looked toward the waiting hands.

“What happens now?”

Needle looked at them too.

Then back at her.

“Best I can tell?”

“What?”

“We see what we're made of.”

Yarn liked that answer.

---

One hand took Needle.

The other gathered Yarn.

And they began.

Yarn felt herself pulled forward.

“Whoa, now. Where we goin'?”

“Easy.”

“Easy for you to say. You ain't the one gettin' pulled.”

Needle turned beneath the guiding fingers.

Yarn crossed over him.

Her eyes widened.

“I'm wrapped around you.”

“Yes.”

“Is that supposed to happen?”

“Yes.”

“You sure?”

“Quite.”

“How you know?”

Needle gave her a look.

“I've been doing this awhile.”

“Well, you could've mentioned that before somebody wrapped me around your neck.”

“I don't have a neck.”

“You know what I mean.”

The hands shifted.

Needle slipped beneath her.

Turned.

Caught her.

Pulled.

And suddenly—

there it was.

A loop.

Yarn went silent.

A tiny loop rested beneath them.

She stared.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“Did we make that?”

Needle looked down.

“We did.”

The hands moved again.

Yarn crossed.

Needle caught her.

Pulled her through.

Another loop appeared.

Then another.

Yarn's nervousness started turning into wonder.

Over.

Catch.

Through.

Again.

At first, they were a mess.

Yarn pulled too tight.

Needle stopped.

“Loosen up.”

“I am loose.”

“You are practically strangling me.”

“I ain't stranglin' nobody.”

“Yarn.”

“All right, all right.”

She relaxed.

Too much.

The next loop nearly fell apart.

Needle sighed.

“Well, now you're barely holding on.”

Yarn huffed.

“You just said loosen up!”

“I didn't say surrender.”

“Oh, you get on my nerves.”

“And yet here we are.”

The hand adjusted Yarn gently.

Not too tight.

Not too loose.

Just enough.

Yarn felt it.

“Oh.”

Needle moved again.

“Feel that?”

“I do.”

“That's tension.”

Yarn considered it.

“Feels more like trust.”

Needle paused.

The hand waited.

For once, Needle had no correction.

Then the rhythm returned.

Over.

Catch.

Through.

Again.

Something changed.

Yarn began to anticipate Needle's movement.

Needle began to understand how Yarn wanted to bend.

The hands guided them both.

But somewhere between those fingers, the silver Needle and the bright orange Yarn began finding a rhythm that belonged to them.

Over.

Catch.

Through.

Again.

One loop became two.

Two became five.

Five became a row.

Yarn stared at what was forming underneath them.

“Well, I'll be.”

Needle looked down too.

“I didn't know I could do that,” she whispered.

“You couldn't.”

Yarn turned toward him.

“Excuse me?”

Needle's eye gleamed.

“Not by yourself.”

Yarn opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Then smiled.

“Well... neither could you.”

“No.”

Needle looked at their growing row.

“I suppose I couldn't.”

The hands continued.

Outside the window, afternoon softened into gold.

The cup beside the chair stopped steaming.

A clock somewhere in the room marked the passing hour.

And Needle and Yarn kept going.

Learning.

Missing.

Correcting.

Trying again.

Finding one another.

---

At last, the hands stopped.

Needle was laid gently on the table.

Yarn settled beside him.

Between them rested their first piece of work.

It wasn't big.

Wasn't fancy.

And Lord knew it wasn't perfect.

One stitch was too tight.

Another leaned sideways.

And near the beginning sat one funny little bump Yarn had been hoping everybody would politely ignore.

Needle looked directly at it.

Yarn groaned.

“Don't.”

“I haven't said anything.”

“You don't have to. You lookin' at it.”

“I am merely observing.”

“You observin' real judgmental.”

Needle tried to hold it in.

He couldn't.

A laugh escaped.

Yarn's eyes widened.

“Well, look at you!”

Needle laughed harder.

“You *can* laugh.”

“I never said I couldn't.”

“Could've fooled me.”

Soon Yarn was laughing too.

And for a little while, neither one cared about that crooked stitch.

Eventually they quieted.

Yarn looked down at their work.

“It ain't perfect.”

“No.”

Her fibers drooped.

Needle looked at her.

Then back at the row.

“But this morning, it didn't exist.”

Yarn became still.

She looked again.

This morning she'd been sitting at the bottom of a shop basket, wondering whether anybody would ever choose her.

Now—

she was becoming something.

Something that hadn't existed until Needle met Yarn.

Until Yarn met Needle.

Until those hands brought them together.

Above them, the hands gently lifted their little piece.

A thumb traveled slowly across the uneven row.

It stopped at the crooked stitch.

Yarn held her breath.

The thumb didn't pull it out.

Didn't fix it.

Didn't hide it.

It simply rubbed across it once.

Gently.

Then the hands folded the work and placed Needle and Yarn side by side inside the old wicker basket.

Footsteps crossed the room.

A light clicked off.

A door closed.

Silence.

One second.

Two.

Three.

Then—

“Would somebody kindly get this thing off my head?”

Yarn jumped.

A folded square of blue fabric rose.

Two polished steel blades poked out.

Then two handles.

Then—

*SNIP!*

A pair of old sewing scissors popped upright.

“Well, good evenin'!”

Yarn rolled backward so fast she bumped straight into Needle.

Scissors opened his blades wide.

“Didn't mean to scare you, sugar.”

“You talk!”

Scissors looked at Needle.

Needle looked at Scissors.

Scissors looked back at Yarn.

“Well, I surely hope so. Been doin' it near forty years.”

From behind them came another voice.

“And ain't nobody figured out how to make him stop yet.”

“Now, don't you start.”

A soft yellow Measuring Tape uncoiled herself from around the side of the basket.

She stretched dramatically.

“Ohhh, mercy. I've been wound up all afternoon.”

Then she noticed Yarn.

“Well, look at you!”

Yarn's eyes moved from Measuring Tape to Scissors to Needle.

“Needle!”

“Yes?”

“You could've told me!”

“I was going to.”

“When?”

“Eventually.”

A tiny blue Stitch Marker swung down from the basket rim.

“Eventually means never with him.”

“Marker,” Needle warned.

“What? I'm tellin' the truth.”

Then the basket seemed to wake all at once.

A wooden Crochet Hook pushed herself out from underneath a ball of cream yarn.

A Thimble rolled upright.

Two little stitch markers started whispering.

A Pin Cushion yawned.

From somewhere deep in the basket, an old wooden Spool called:

“What's all that racket up there?”

“We got somebody new!” Marker shouted.

“Well, why didn't somebody say so?”

“I just did!”

And just like that, the whole basket erupted.

“New yarn?”

“What color?”

“Move over!”

“Let me see!”

“Quit pushin'!”

“I ain't pushin' nobody!”

Yarn pressed herself against Needle.

“Is it always like this?”

“No.”

Needle paused.

“Sometimes they're louder.”

Yarn stared at him.

Then she laughed.

She couldn't help it.

This place was ridiculous.

And wonderful.

Scissors stepped forward as formally as a pair of scissors possibly could.

“All right, everybody, hush up. Y'all act like ain't nobody ever come home before.”

The basket quieted.

Mostly.

Scissors turned toward Yarn and gave a little bow.

“Name's Scissors.”

He opened and closed his blades.

*Snip-snip.*

“I handle endings.”

Measuring Tape leaned toward Yarn.

“And sometimes things that ain't supposed to end.”

“Don't confuse the girl.”

“I'm Measuring Tape, sweetheart. If you ever need to know whether somethin' is too long, too short, too wide, too narrow—”

Scissors groaned.

“Here we go.”

“—or whether somebody's been sneakin' extra stitches into a row—”

“I did that one time.”

“Seven.”

“One project.”

“Seven stitches.”

Yarn giggled.

Marker hopped down.

“I'm Marker. I help everybody remember where they supposed to be.”

Old Spool called from below:

“Then how come you always gettin' lost?”

The whole basket laughed.

Marker crossed his tiny plastic arms.

“That ain't funny.”

“It was a little funny,” Yarn said.

Marker tried not to smile.

Failed.

One by one, the others introduced themselves.

Hook.

Thimble.

Pins.

Buttons.

Spool.

Little scraps of yarn left from projects completed years ago.

Every one of them seemed to have a story.

Yarn looked around the basket.

Back at the shop she'd been one skein among hundreds.

Here—

everybody knew everybody.

She turned toward Needle.

“You live with all these folks?”

Needle nodded.

“For a long time.”

Scissors corrected him.

“*We* live with *him*.”

Needle sighed.

“And that,” Scissors continued, “is why we deserve everybody's sympathy.”

Another round of laughter filled the basket.

Yarn laughed hardest of all.

Then—

footsteps.

Everything stopped.

Instantly.

Scissors dropped.

Marker hooked himself onto the basket.

Measuring Tape collapsed into a loose coil.

Hook fell sideways.

Needle became perfectly still.

Yarn looked around wildly.

“What happened?”

Without moving, Needle whispered:

“Leola.”

“Who?”

The shadow passed over them.

The familiar hand reached down and moved a few things inside the basket.

Yarn became still.

The hand withdrew.

Footsteps moved away again.

They waited.

A door closed.

Scissors slowly raised one blade.

“Coast clear.”

Life returned.

Yarn looked toward Needle.

“That's her name?”

“Yes.”

“Leola?”

Needle's voice softened.

“Miss Leola.”

Yarn looked toward the doorway where the woman had disappeared.

“She always this gentle?”

Needle glanced around at the others.

Scissors answered first.

“That woman takes care of everything she touches.”

Measuring Tape nodded.

“Been that way as long as I've known her.”

“How long's that?”

Measuring Tape began unrolling proudly.

“Well—”

Scissors snapped his blades.

“Don't ask her unless you got the rest of the night.”

“I heard that.”

Hook laughed.

Yarn looked around.

“So y'all really know her?”

Everybody started talking at once.

“Know her?”

“Lord, child—”

“We could tell you some stories.”

“Remember that yellow blanket?”

“The yellow blanket? What about that green sweater?”

“That sleeve was crooked.”

“It most certainly was not.”

“It leaned.”

“It was *shaped*.”

Old Spool's deep voice rose from the bottom.

“Let the girl breathe.”

The basket settled.

Mostly.

Yarn smiled.

“What kinda stories?”

Scissors moved beside her.

“Well, Miss Leola been makin' things longer than some of us been in this basket.”

“Longer than *you*?” Yarn asked.

Scissors opened his blades indignantly.

“Watch yourself, young lady.”

Measuring Tape chuckled.

“She learned the same way everybody learns.”

Yarn looked surprised.

“What you mean?”

“Made mistakes.”

Hook nodded.

“Dropped stitches.”

“Pulled some too tight,” Measuring Tape added.

“Missed some altogether,” Marker said.

Scissors leaned closer.

“And once—”

Needle interrupted.

“Don't.”

Yarn's eyes widened.

“Oh, now you gotta tell me.”

Scissors grinned.

“She worked half a row before realizing she'd been followin' the wrong part of the pattern.”

Yarn gasped dramatically.

“Miss Leola?”

“The very same.”

“What'd she do?”

Scissors shrugged his handles.

“Pulled it out.”

Yarn stared.

“All of it?”

“Every stitch.”

“But she'd already done all that work.”

Old Spool spoke softly.

“Work ain't wasted just because you gotta do it again.”

The basket quieted.

Yarn looked toward him.

Old Spool continued.

“First time teaches your hands what don't work. Next time, your hands know a little more.”

Yarn looked down at the crooked row she'd made with Needle.

Measuring Tape nudged her gently.

“That's how Miss Leola learned.”

Hook smiled.

“And that's how you're gonna learn.”

Yarn studied the uneven stitches.

For the first time, she didn't wish the crooked one would disappear.

“So what does she make?”

That question woke everybody up again.

“Oh, honey.”

“Blankets!”

“Hats!”

“Scarves!”

“Dolls!”

“Baby clothes!”

“Don't forget them mittens.”

Scissors pointed toward the chair.

“See that blanket?”

Yarn peeked over the basket.

A folded blanket rested across the back of Leola's chair.

“Miss Leola made that?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

Hook pointed toward a shelf.

“And that little doll.”

Yarn turned.

“Her too?”

“Her too.”

Marker bounced excitedly.

“She makes things for babies!”

Measuring Tape added, “Things for family.”

Hook said, “Things for friends.”

Scissors grew quieter.

“And sometimes things for folks who need a little warmth and ain't got nobody else makin' it for 'em.”

Yarn looked around the room again.

Everything felt different now.

The blanket wasn't simply a blanket.

The doll wasn't simply a doll.

The basket wasn't simply a basket.

They were evidence.

Of time.

Of learning.

Of somebody sitting in that same chair, over and over, turning ordinary thread into something another person could hold.

Yarn looked down at herself.

“So why'd she choose me?”

Nobody answered immediately.

Finally Needle said:

“We don't know.”

Yarn turned toward him.

“You don't?”

“No.”

Scissors smiled.

“That's half the fun, sugar.”

Measuring Tape curled herself comfortably beside Yarn.

“Sometimes we don't find out till the work starts tellin' us.”

“The work talks too?”

Everybody stared at Yarn.

Then the whole basket burst out laughing.

Yarn looked offended.

“What?”

Scissors could barely close his blades.

“Oh, sweetheart, you gon' fit in just fine.”

---

Later that night, the house grew completely still.

One by one, the basket folk settled down.

Marker fell asleep hanging halfway off the rim.

Measuring Tape curled herself into a neat spiral.

Hook tucked beneath a folded square.

Scissors closed his blades.

Old Spool stopped telling a story somewhere in the middle of it.

Soon only Needle and Yarn remained awake.

Yarn looked toward the window.

Milwaukee lights glowed beyond the glass.

She had never seen a city at night.

“So that's outside?”

Needle moved beside her.

“Part of it.”

“Big?”

“Very.”

“Bigger than the yarn shop?”

Needle smiled.

“Considerably.”

Yarn thought about that.

“This morning I thought that shop was the whole world.”

“Most things do, until they leave where they started.”

Yarn grew quiet.

After a moment she asked:

“You ever wonder what you gonna become?”

Needle looked toward their unfinished row.

“I don't become anything.”

“What you mean?”

“I'm a needle.”

“So?”

“So I help make the thing.”

Yarn looked down at herself.

“And I become it.”

Needle hesitated.

“In a way.”

That troubled her.

“If I become a scarf or a blanket or whatever Miss Leola makin'... then what happens to me?”

Needle hadn't expected that question.

Yarn's voice grew smaller.

“Do I stop bein' Yarn?”

From somewhere below them came Old Spool's sleepy voice.

“No.”

They both looked down.

They had thought he was asleep.

Old Spool continued without opening his eyes.

“You don't stop bein' what you were just because you become somethin' more.”

Yarn said nothing.

“Every stitch still you,” he murmured. “Just arranged into somethin' with purpose.”

A moment later, he began snoring.

Yarn looked at Needle.

“He always do that?”

“Give wisdom and immediately fall asleep?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“Constantly.”

Yarn giggled.

Then she looked at their first row again.

“I think I'm scared.”

Needle moved closer.

“Of becoming something?”

“Of not bein' good at it.”

Needle considered her words.

Then he looked at the crooked stitch.

“You see that one?”

Yarn groaned.

“Everybody in this house done pointed that stitch out.”

“Look at the one beside it.”

She did.

“What about it?”

“It's better.”

Yarn looked closer.

It was.

“And the one after that?”

Better still.

Needle said quietly:

“You were learning while we made them.”

Yarn stared at the row.

The mistakes hadn't interrupted the progress.

They **were the progress**.

A little smile appeared.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“You think we gon' make somethin' beautiful?”

From across the basket, Scissors suddenly shouted with his eyes still closed:

“NOT WITH THAT TENSION!”

“SCISSORS!”

The whole basket woke up laughing.

Marker fell off the rim.

Measuring Tape uncoiled herself in surprise.

Hook yelled, “What happened?”

Scissors was laughing so hard his blades rattled.

Even Needle couldn't hold himself together.

Yarn tried to stay mad.

She lasted maybe three seconds.

Then she laughed too.

The sound filled the basket.

Filled the little room.

Filled the quiet old house.

Eventually everybody settled again.

This time Needle leaned close enough that only Yarn could hear him.

“I think we already started.”

Yarn smiled.

Outside the window, Milwaukee slept beneath the night sky.

Inside, the old house settled around them.

And beside Leola's chair sat a worn wicker basket that looked, to anybody passing by, like nothing more than a collection of ordinary crafting things.

But it wasn't.

It held Scissors and his stories.

Measuring Tape and her memories.

Marker, who could remember everybody's place except his own.

Hook.

Thimble.

Pins.

Buttons.

Old Spool.

Needle.

And now Yarn.

A little family of things that had spent years inside Leola's hands.

Tomorrow those hands would return.

There would be more loops.

More mistakes.

More laughter.

There would be projects they couldn't yet imagine.

Seasons they hadn't yet seen.

Things would be made.

Things would be undone.

Things would break.

And some broken things would find their way back home.

But tonight none of them knew any of that.

Tonight Yarn knew only three things.

She had been chosen.

She had found a home.

And somewhere between **over, catch, through, again**, she had met somebody she very much wanted to know better.

She glanced toward Needle.

He was still smiling.

Yarn closed her eyes.

And for the first time in her life, she fell asleep wondering not where she would go—

but what they might become.

**Thread by thread.**

**Stitch by stitch.**

# *Needle & Yarn: A Love Stitched in Time*

# Chapter Two — The First Hello

Morning came quietly to Leola's house.

First came a thin ribbon of sunlight slipping through the curtains.

Then the birds started fussing outside.

Then the old clock on the wall decided everybody had slept long enough.

*Tick.*

*Tock.*

*Tick.*

*Tock.*

Inside the wicker basket, nobody moved.

Not Scissors.

Not Measuring Tape.

Not Hook.

Not Marker.

Not even Yarn.

For nearly five whole seconds.

Then—

“Needle?”

Nothing.

“Needle.”

Silence.

“Needle, you sleep?”

“No.”

Yarn waited.

“Then why you ain't answer me?”

“Because I was hoping you'd go back to sleep.”

Yarn gasped.

“Well, good mornin' to you too.”

From somewhere beneath them came Scissors' sleepy voice.

“For the love of buttons, please don't start this early.”

“I was just askin' a question.”

Needle sighed.

“You've been asking questions since you arrived.”

“Well, how else I'm supposed to learn anything?”

“Observation.”

“Observation?”

“Yes.”

Yarn thought about that.

“All right.”

Silence returned.

Needle relaxed.

Five seconds passed.

Yarn whispered:

“What we observin'?”

Scissors burst out laughing.

Needle groaned.

And just like that, the basket was awake.

---

Measuring Tape stretched herself along the side.

“Ohhh, mercy.”

Hook rolled over.

Marker climbed onto the rim and nearly fell outside.

“Whoa!”

Scissors caught him with the tip of one handle.

“Every mornin'.”

“I had it.”

“You absolutely did not.”

Old Spool grumbled from somewhere below.

“Can't a respectable spool get a decent night's sleep in his own basket?”

“You snored all night,” Measuring Tape said.

“I was meditating.”

“You was rattlin' the buttons.”

Yarn laughed.

She looked around at everybody.

Last night, she'd fallen asleep wondering whether any of this had really happened.

Talking scissors.

An opinionated measuring tape.

A stitch marker who couldn't stay where he was supposed to.

Old Spool.

Hook.

And Needle.

Especially Needle.

Morning had arrived, and somehow the impossible already felt like home.

Yarn peeked over the basket.

“Where Miss Leola?”

“Kitchen,” Hook said.

“How you know?”

Scissors yawned.

“Because it's morning.”

“That don't explain nothin'.”

“It does around here.”

Right on cue, sounds drifted through the house.

A cupboard opened.

Water ran.

A spoon touched the side of a cup.

*Clink.*

*Clink.*

Then came the smell.

Yarn inhaled.

“Ohhh.”

Measuring Tape smiled.

“Coffee.”

Yarn inhaled again.

“And somethin' else.”

Old Spool spoke from below.

“Toast.”

Scissors raised one blade thoughtfully.

“Could be biscuits.”

Hook shook her head.

“Weekday.”

Scissors lowered his blade.

“Toast.”

Yarn looked from one to another.

“How in the world y'all know all this?”

Marker puffed himself up.

“We know Miss Leola.”

Scissors nodded.

“You stay around somebody long enough, sugar, you learn their rhythm.”

Yarn glanced toward Needle.

He was looking toward the kitchen.

“Their rhythm?”

Needle nodded.

“You'll see.”

---

Footsteps approached.

Instantly—

stillness.

Yarn remembered this time.

Barely.

The room became ordinary again.

Leola entered.

From inside the basket, Yarn couldn't see all of her.

She saw pieces.

A sleeve.

The edge of her clothing.

The familiar hands.

Those hands opened the curtains wider.

Morning flooded into the room.

One hand lifted the cup from the table.

The other straightened a little stack of papers.

Then Leola sat.

The chair gave its familiar little creak.

Yarn felt Needle beside her become attentive.

The hand reached down.

Not for him.

For Yarn.

“Oh!”

She rose.

The other hand found Needle.

They met again above the basket.

Yarn whispered:

“Well, good mornin'.”

Needle whispered back:

“You already said that.”

“Not properly.”

Needle almost smiled.

Then the hands began.

---

Their crooked little first row from yesterday was still there.

Yarn looked at it.

“Oh, Lord.”

“What?”

“She kept it.”

“Of course she kept it.”

“I thought maybe she'd pull it out.”

Needle looked at her.

“Why?”

“Because it ain't right.”

The hands positioned them.

Needle answered quietly:

“It isn't finished.”

Yarn looked down at the row.

There was a difference between those two things.

She liked that.

The hand guided Needle.

Yarn moved.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

**Again.**

Yesterday, every movement had surprised her.

Today—

something had changed.

She remembered.

Not perfectly.

But enough.

Needle turned.

Yarn anticipated him.

He caught her.

She loosened before he had to tell her.

A loop formed.

Needle noticed.

Yarn noticed him noticing.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You lookin' at me.”

“I am working.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

They continued.

Another stitch.

Then another.

Yarn grinned.

“I did that one right.”

“It was acceptable.”

“Acceptable?”

“Quite.”

“Needle, that stitch is beautiful.”

“It is a stitch.”

“It is a *beautiful* stitch.”

Needle gave the smallest sigh.

“Yes, Yarn. It is a beautiful stitch.”

“I knew it.”

---

A little later, Yarn became adventurous.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“What if we do it this way?”

“What way?”

“This way.”

She shifted unexpectedly.

Needle nearly slipped.

“Yarn!”

“What?”

“That is not the stitch.”

“How you know if we ain't tried it?”

“Because stitches have structure.”

“And structures can't change?”

“That is not what I said.”

“Sounded like it.”

The hands corrected her.

Yarn huffed.

Needle continued.

“You cannot simply go wherever you please.”

“Why not?”

“Because we're making something.”

“Exactly!”

Needle stopped.

Yarn continued.

“What if what we makin' wants to be different?”

“Projects do not *want* things.”

From the basket, Scissors' muffled voice somehow reached them.

“HE GON' LEARN.”

Needle's eye narrowed toward the basket.

Yarn tried not to laugh.

Failed.

The hand moved again.

Needle said quietly:

“Focus.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Don't call me that.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Yarn.”

“Yes, sir, Mister Needle, sir.”

Needle groaned.

Yarn laughed so hard she nearly lost her tension.

The hand gently corrected it.

And for just a moment—

Needle laughed too.

Yarn heard him.

She said nothing.

But something warm moved through her fibers.

---

The morning went on.

They worked.

They fussed.

They corrected each other.

Yarn learned that Needle liked order.

Needle learned that Yarn would question absolutely everything.

Yarn discovered that Needle knew more than he let on.

Needle discovered that Yarn sometimes found solutions precisely because she didn't know what she was “supposed” to do.

And slowly their little piece grew.

At one point the hands stopped.

Leola lifted their work.

Turned it.

Looked at it.

Her thumb moved over the stitches.

Yarn became nervous.

“What she doin'?”

“Checking our work.”

“Oh.”

The thumb stopped over one stitch.

Yarn whispered:

“Which one she lookin' at?”

Needle looked.

“Yours.”

“They all mine!”

“Precisely.”

“Needle!”

The hand moved again.

No unraveling.

Yarn relaxed.

Then something unexpected happened.

Leola hummed.

Just softly.

Barely louder than the clock.

Yarn became still.

“What is that?”

Needle listened.

“She does that sometimes.”

“Why?”

“When she's happy.”

Yarn listened.

The melody had no words.

Just warmth.

The hands resumed.

Yarn looked down at the fabric growing beneath them.

“She happy with us?”

Needle considered that.

“I believe so.”

Yarn brightened.

“Well, look at us.”

Needle smiled.

“Yes.”

“Look at us.”

---

That afternoon, Leola carried their work to the window.

Sunlight poured across the stitches.

Yarn could finally see what they had been making.

It wasn't merely a row anymore.

There were several.

Not enough to tell exactly what the finished thing would become.

But enough to see that it was becoming **something**.

Yarn stared.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“We made all that?”

“With considerable assistance.”

Yarn looked at the hands.

“Well, naturally.”

Needle followed her gaze.

The hands rested for a moment.

Then they began again.

Yarn watched them differently now.

Yesterday she'd thought the hands were simply moving them.

Today she noticed something else.

They listened.

Not with ears.

With touch.

When Yarn tightened, the fingers loosened.

When Needle caught at the wrong angle, the wrist shifted.

When the stitch needed more room, the hands gave it room.

There was knowledge inside those movements.

Years of it.

Yarn whispered:

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“You think Miss Leola learned us before we learned each other?”

Needle considered the question.

“I think she's still learning too.”

Yarn looked surprised.

“After all this time?”

“Especially after all this time.”

Yarn smiled.

She liked that answer even more.

---

Eventually Leola stopped.

The hands laid Needle and Yarn beside the growing work.

The chair creaked as she stood.

Footsteps crossed the room.

The light remained on.

Nobody moved.

They waited.

A door opened somewhere else in the house.

Then closed.

One second.

Two.

Three.

*SNIP!*

“REPORT!”

Scissors shot upright.

Yarn jumped.

“Lord, Scissors!”

“What?”

“You gon' make me unravel myself one day!”

Measuring Tape rose more gracefully.

“How'd it go, sweetheart?”

Before Yarn could answer, Marker bounced forward.

“Did you make something?”

“Yes.”

“Did you mess up?”

“Yes.”

“Did Needle get mad?”

“YES.”

“I did not get mad.”

Everybody looked at Needle.

Hook raised an eyebrow.

Scissors opened one blade.

Needle sighed.

“I became mildly frustrated.”

Yarn laughed.

“That man was two stitches away from losin' his whole religion.”

The basket erupted.

Needle stared at her.

“My what?”

“Don't worry about it.”

Scissors could barely speak.

“Oh, I like her.”

“I do too,” Marker said.

Needle glanced toward Yarn.

She didn't notice.

Measuring Tape did.

She said nothing.

But she smiled.

---

Hook inspected the growing piece.

“Better tension.”

Yarn beamed.

“You see that?”

“I do.”

“Needle taught me.”

Needle looked surprised.

Yarn continued.

“And I taught him how not to be so stiff.”

“I beg your pardon.”

Scissors laughed.

“She got you there.”

Needle looked toward Hook for support.

Hook suddenly became fascinated with the bottom of the basket.

“Coward,” Needle muttered.

Old Spool chuckled.

Measuring Tape examined the work.

“You know what I see?”

Yarn leaned forward.

“What?”

“Yesterday's stitches.”

Yarn's smile faded.

“The bad ones?”

“The first ones.”

Measuring Tape pointed farther along.

“And today's.”

Yarn looked.

The difference was obvious.

Yesterday's stitches wandered.

Today's sat more evenly.

Not perfect.

Better.

Old Spool spoke.

“That's why you don't hide where you started.”

Yarn looked down at the work.

“So you can see how far you came.”

Old Spool smiled.

“Now you gettin' it.”

---

Marker hopped onto the work.

“What's it gonna be?”

Scissors shrugged.

“Too early.”

“A scarf?”

“Maybe.”

“A hat?”

“No.”

“A blanket?”

“Could be.”

Marker gasped.

“A DRAGON?”

Everybody stared at him.

“What?”

Scissors shook his handles.

“Child, go sit down somewhere.”

Marker wandered away muttering:

“Could've been a dragon.”

Yarn laughed.

Then looked at Needle.

“What you think?”

“About what?”

“What we're makin'.”

“I don't know.”

“You ain't curious?”

“Of course.”

“Then guess.”

“I don't guess.”

Yarn stared at him.

“You don't guess?”

“No.”

“You don't wonder?”

“I wonder.”

“But you don't guess.”

“Correct.”

Yarn shook herself.

“Needle, you need some adventure in your life.”

“And you need considerably less.”

“Well, good thing we found each other then.”

The words came out before Yarn thought about them.

Silence.

Needle looked at her.

Yarn looked at Needle.

Scissors slowly turned toward Measuring Tape.

Measuring Tape slowly turned toward Scissors.

Marker whispered much too loudly:

“Oooooooooh.”

Yarn spun toward him.

“Hush!”

“I ain't say nothin'!”

“You literally just said somethin'!”

Needle tried to look anywhere else.

Scissors leaned toward Measuring Tape.

“This gon' be interesting.”

“Oh, leave 'em alone.”

“I ain't botherin' nobody.”

“You planning to.”

“Well, that's different.”

---

That evening, Leola returned.

Stillness.

She sat again.

The hands reached down.

Needle and Yarn rose together.

This time Yarn wasn't nervous.

She knew the rhythm.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

**Again.**

But something else happened.

Yarn stopped thinking so hard about each movement.

Her body began remembering.

Needle turned before she expected it—

and she was ready.

Yarn loosened—

Needle moved through.

They missed a stitch.

Went back.

Tried again.

Got it.

No argument.

Another.

Another.

The room grew darker.

A lamp clicked on.

Golden light replaced daylight.

Leola's hands kept moving.

And she began humming again.

Yarn listened.

Needle listened.

Their rhythm settled into hers.

Three rhythms becoming one.

Hand.

Needle.

Yarn.

The fabric grew.

And for the first time, Yarn understood what Scissors had meant.

You stayed around somebody long enough—

you learned their rhythm.

---

Later, Leola placed them back into the basket.

The house became quiet.

The others woke.

But this time nobody immediately started talking.

They'd all seen it.

Something had changed.

Scissors finally spoke.

“Well.”

Measuring Tape smiled.

“Mmm-hmm.”

Hook nodded.

“Mmm-hmm.”

Marker looked around.

“What?”

Nobody answered.

“What?!”

Scissors leaned toward him.

“You'll understand when you're older.”

“I'm plastic! I don't get older!”

That broke everybody.

The basket filled with laughter.

Eventually the others settled down.

Yarn rested beside Needle.

Their growing work lay folded above them.

She looked toward it.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“I think I know what I like about makin' things.”

“What?”

“This mornin', that part wasn't there.”

“No.”

“Then we worked.”

“Yes.”

“We messed some up.”

“Several.”

Yarn nudged him.

“Don't ruin my moment.”

“My apologies.”

“And now it's there.”

Needle looked at the fabric.

“Yes.”

Yarn's voice softened.

“We made somethin' today that the world didn't have yesterday.”

Needle looked at her.

For once, he had no clever correction.

“No,” he said quietly.

“It didn't.”

Yarn settled closer.

“That's a good feelin'.”

“It is.”

A little silence passed.

Then Yarn whispered:

“You know what else?”

Needle braced himself.

“What?”

“I think you like me.”

Needle went completely still.

From across the basket came the unmistakable sound of Scissors trying not to laugh.

Needle lowered his voice.

“Scissors.”

“I ain't said nothin'.”

Yarn grinned.

“You didn't answer.”

“I believe it's time to sleep.”

“That ain't an answer either.”

“Good night, Yarn.”

“Needle.”

“Good night.”

“Needle.”

Silence.

Yarn smiled.

“All right then.”

She turned away.

“Good night.”

A few moments passed.

Yarn closed her eyes.

Then Needle's quiet voice came through the darkness.

“Yarn?”

Her eyes opened.

“Yes?”

A pause.

“I am glad Miss Leola chose you.”

Yarn didn't tease him.

Didn't laugh.

Didn't even turn around.

She simply smiled into the darkness.

“Me too.”

Across the basket, Scissors whispered:

“I knew it.”

Measuring Tape slapped him with one end of herself.

“Ow!”

“Hush and let them be.”

Yarn giggled.

Needle groaned.

And somewhere beneath all of them, Old Spool chuckled in his sleep.

Outside the window, Milwaukee settled into another quiet night.

Inside Leola's basket, yesterday's strangers rested beside today's growing stitches.

They didn't know yet what they were making.

They didn't know what the coming seasons would ask of them.

They certainly didn't know how much learning to create together would change them.

But they had learned something important.

A good stitch wasn't made by Needle alone.

It wasn't made by Yarn alone.

And it wasn't made by pulling harder whenever something went wrong.

It came from learning when to hold.

When to loosen.

When to follow.

When to guide.

And when two very different things finally learned one another's rhythm—

something new could begin.

Something neither one could make alone.

**Thread by thread.**

**Stitch by stitch.**

**Together.**

### — End of Chapter Two —


# Chapter Three — Working Together

Rain had been tapping against Leola's window since before daylight.

Not hard.

Just steady.

*Tap.*

*Tap-tap.*

*Tap.*

The kind of rain that made a house feel warmer simply because you were inside it.

In the wicker basket beside Leola's chair, Yarn was awake.

Needle wasn't.

Or at least he was pretending not to be.

Yarn stared at him.

Then at the ceiling.

Then back at him.

She rolled a little closer.

Nothing.

Closer.

Nothing.

Just a little—

“Yarn.”

She stopped.

“Yes?”

“You are approximately one inch from my face.”

“Oh.”

She rolled backward.

A little.

“Better?”

“No.”

She rolled another half inch.

“How 'bout now?”

Needle opened one eye.

“You have an entire basket.”

“I got cold.”

“You are yarn.”

“That don't mean I can't be cold.”

Needle stared at her.

Yarn stared back.

From underneath them came Scissors' voice.

“Would y'all either fall in love or fall asleep? Some of us is tryin' to rest.”

Yarn nearly unraveled.

“SCISSORS!”

Needle sat straight up.

“We are doing no such thing.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

Measuring Tape whispered:

“Leave them babies alone.”

“I didn't bother nobody.”

“You announced their business to the whole basket.”

“Ain't no business to announce.”

Needle nodded firmly.

“Precisely.”

Yarn looked at him.

“Well, you ain't gotta agree *that* fast.”

Needle blinked.

“I—”

Scissors started laughing.

Measuring Tape groaned.

“Lord, give me strength.”

---

Footsteps.

Instant silence.

Leola entered carrying her coffee.

The rain followed her in the sound of little drops against the window.

She set the cup beside her chair.

Sat.

And reached into the basket.

Yarn felt those familiar fingers close around her.

Needle rose in the other hand.

They met above the basket.

Yarn whispered:

“Mornin'.”

“Good morning.”

“You sleep good?”

“I did until somebody invaded my personal space.”

“I told you I was cold.”

“You are still yarn.”

Yarn frowned.

“You keep bringin' that up like it's relevant.”

The hands positioned them.

Needle turned.

Yarn crossed.

And their morning began.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

**Again.**

---

Something had changed.

Neither of them said it.

But they both felt it.

Leola's hands didn't have to correct them nearly as much anymore.

Needle knew when Yarn was coming.

Yarn knew where Needle needed her.

When he turned, she loosened.

When she crossed, he caught her.

When a loop resisted, neither pulled harder.

They adjusted.

Together.

The rhythm had become almost effortless.

Almost.

“Too tight.”

“There you go again.”

“It is.”

“I know what I'm doin'.”

Needle stopped.

“You have been crocheting for two days.”

“And?”

“And yesterday you attempted to invent a stitch.”

“It could've worked.”

“It formed a knot.”

“A creative knot.”

“It took Miss Leola three minutes to undo it.”

Yarn lowered her voice.

“She ain't gotta know we callin' it a knot.”

Needle laughed.

The hands continued.

The fabric beneath them had grown considerably.

Yarn looked down.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“I think it's long.”

“It is.”

“How long?”

From the basket came Measuring Tape's muffled voice.

“FINALLY SOMEBODY NEED ME.”

Yarn burst out laughing.

Leola's hand paused.

Needle whispered:

“You're going to get us caught.”

“She can't hear me.”

“You don't know that.”

Yarn became very still.

She looked toward Leola.

Needle waited.

Yarn whispered:

“You think she can?”

Needle looked at the hands holding them.

“I've wondered.”

That silenced Yarn.

For about four seconds.

“Ask her.”

“I am not asking Miss Leola whether she can hear her yarn talking.”

“Why?”

“What precisely would you like me to say?”

Yarn lowered her voice into a serious imitation of him.

“Pardon me, Miss Leola, but might I inquire whether you've been listenin' to our private conversations?”

Needle stared at her.

Yarn grinned.

“That's not what I sound like.”

“Sounded just like you.”

“It did not.”

The hands began moving again.

Yarn laughed.

And Needle—

despite himself—

laughed with her.

---

Later that morning, Leola stopped.

She lifted the fabric.

Turned it lengthwise.

Yarn tried to see.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“What are we makin'?”

“I still don't know.”

“It's long.”

“Observant.”

“And narrow.”

“Also observant.”

Yarn gasped.

“A scarf.”

Needle looked again.

“It may be.”

“A scarf!”

The idea thrilled her.

“We makin' a scarf!”

“Possibly.”

“Needle, have some joy.”

“I have joy.”

“Where?”

Needle ignored her.

Yarn looked at the growing fabric.

A scarf.

A real thing.

Something somebody could wear.

Then she noticed something.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“If that's a scarf...”

She hesitated.

“Then that's me.”

Needle looked down.

Much of the original skein was gone now.

Not gone exactly.

Changed.

Row after row of sunset-orange Yarn had become fabric.

She looked at herself.

Then at the scarf.

Then herself again.

Her excitement softened.

“Oh.”

Needle understood.

“You're thinking about what Old Spool said.”

Yarn nodded.

“I know he said I don't stop bein' me.”

“No.”

“But look how much of me ain't in the basket no more.”

Needle was quiet.

“What happens when all of me is over there?”

Needle didn't have an answer.

For the first time since they'd met, Yarn wasn't joking.

And for the first time, Needle couldn't fix the problem by explaining the proper way to make a stitch.

He looked toward the growing scarf.

Then back at what remained of Yarn.

“I don't know.”

She looked at him.

Needle hated saying those words.

Yarn knew that.

“You don't?”

“No.”

The hands rested.

Rain whispered against the window.

Needle moved as close to Yarn as Leola's fingers allowed.

“But wherever you end up…”

Yarn waited.

Needle continued.

“…I'll know where every stitch came from.”

Her fibers went very still.

“You would?”

“I was there.”

Yarn looked down at the scarf.

“So if I become that…”

“Yes.”

“…you'd still know me?”

Needle answered without hesitation.

“I would know you anywhere.”

Yarn looked at him.

And something happened between them that neither had words for yet.

The rain kept falling.

The clock kept ticking.

Leola lifted her coffee.

But for Yarn, the whole room seemed to become very quiet.

Needle finally looked away.

“We should continue.”

Yarn's voice came softer than usual.

“Yeah.”

The hands returned.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

**Again.**

Only now the rhythm felt different.

---

By afternoon, the rain had become heavier.

The room darkened enough that Leola switched on the lamp beside her chair.

Golden light fell across her hands.

The scarf had grown.

Yarn had become quieter.

Needle noticed.

“You all right?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“That is not an answer.”

Yarn looked at him.

“You been around Measuring Tape too long.”

“She has certain useful qualities.”

“Don't ever tell her that.”

“I have no intention of doing so.”

Yarn smiled.

Then the smile faded.

“What if whoever gets me don't like me?”

Needle stopped.

“What?”

“The scarf.”

“They will.”

“You don't know that.”

“No.”

“What if they think the color too bright?”

“They're wrong.”

Yarn looked surprised.

Needle continued.

“What?”

“Nothin'.”

“What?”

“You didn't even think about that.”

“There was nothing to think about.”

Yarn smiled.

“So you like my color?”

Needle realized too late what he'd done.

“I didn't say—”

“You like my color.”

“I said anyone who disliked it would be incorrect.”

“That's the same thing.”

“It is not.”

“Needle likes my color.”

“Yarn.”

“Needle thinks I'm pretty.”

“I said no such thing.”

Scissors' voice drifted faintly from the basket.

“HE DO.”

Needle closed his eye.

Yarn laughed so hard Leola had to adjust her tension.

---

But then—

something went wrong.

Needle entered the next stitch.

Yarn crossed.

The hook of the movement caught.

Too tight.

Needle pulled.

Yarn resisted.

“Wait.”

“I have it.”

“No, you don't.”

Needle pulled again.

The loop tightened.

“Needle.”

“I can fix it.”

“Stop pullin'!”

The stitch cinched down.

Needle strained.

Yarn felt herself twist.

“YOU HURTIN' ME!”

Needle froze.

Leola's hands froze too.

Silence.

Needle released the pressure immediately.

Yarn loosened.

The loop remained ugly and tight.

Neither spoke.

Leola examined it.

Then her fingers did something Yarn hadn't experienced before.

They began undoing the stitch.

Yarn felt herself being pulled backward.

“No!”

Needle watched.

The previous stitch came undone too.

“She's tearin' it up!”

“No. Wait.”

Another stitch disappeared.

Yarn panicked.

“Needle, she's undoing us!”

Needle looked carefully at Leola's hands.

“No.”

“How you gon' say no? I can see it!”

“Yarn, look.”

The hands stopped.

Three stitches had been removed.

The mistake was gone.

Everything before it remained.

Then Leola repositioned them.

Yarn stared.

“She only took out the bad part.”

Needle nodded.

The hands began again.

Slowly.

Needle entered.

Yarn crossed.

Neither rushed.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

The new stitch settled into place.

Clean.

Even.

Yarn stared.

Needle whispered:

“Better.”

Yarn looked at him.

“You could've just listened to me.”

Needle's satisfaction disappeared.

He knew she was right.

“I could have.”

“You thought you had to fix it.”

“Yes.”

“By yourself.”

Needle looked down.

“Yes.”

Yarn waited for an excuse.

None came.

Needle said:

“I'm sorry.”

Yarn softened.

“You scared me.”

“I know.”

“And you hurt.”

Needle looked up.

“I know.”

She moved slightly closer.

“Next time I say stop…”

“I stop.”

No argument.

No explanation.

Yarn nodded.

“All right.”

The hands moved again.

Their rhythm returned.

Not quite the same rhythm.

Something better.

Because this one contained something the old rhythm hadn't needed yet.

**Forgiveness.**

---

That evening, the rain finally stopped.

Leola finished the last row.

The hands paused.

Needle felt it first.

Yarn did too.

Something was different.

Leola reached for Scissors.

Inside the basket—

Scissors' eyes widened.

“Oh.”

Measuring Tape looked at him.

“What?”

Scissors became solemn.

“It's time.”

Yarn heard him.

“Time for what?”

Nobody answered.

Leola lifted Scissors.

He became still in her hand.

Yarn looked at Needle.

“Needle?”

He understood.

The scarf was finished.

And Yarn was still connected to the skein.

“No.”

Her voice trembled.

“Needle?”

He moved toward her.

“It's all right.”

“What he about to do?”

“Yarn—”

“Needle, what he about to do?”

Scissors opened.

Yarn saw the blades.

For the first time, Scissors wasn't funny.

Leola positioned him against the strand connecting Yarn to the finished scarf.

Yarn panicked.

“No.”

Needle spoke quickly.

“Listen to me.”

“NO.”

“Yarn.”

“Don't let him cut me!”

Needle couldn't move.

Leola held him on the table.

Scissors couldn't speak.

None of them could.

The blades closed.

*Snip.*

And Yarn screamed.

---

Silence.

She waited for pain.

For darkness.

For herself to disappear.

Nothing happened.

Yarn opened her eyes.

She was still there.

Smaller.

But there.

And across the table—

the scarf remained.

Part of her.

Separate from her.

But still her.

Leola's fingers tied off the final strand.

Needle looked between Yarn and the scarf.

“Yarn?”

She didn't answer.

“Yarn.”

Her voice shook.

“I'm here.”

Needle's whole body relaxed.

“You're here.”

Yarn looked at the scarf.

“So is she.”

Needle understood immediately.

The scarf wasn't *Yarn*.

And yet—

it was.

Something Yarn had become without ceasing to be herself.

Leola lifted the finished scarf.

Held it against the light.

And smiled.

Yarn saw the smile.

Her fear slowly gave way to something else.

Pride.

“Oh, my stars.”

Needle looked at the scarf.

The crooked first stitches were still there.

So were yesterday's better ones.

And today's.

Their arguments.

Their laughter.

Their mistake.

The three stitches they'd undone and remade.

All of it had become part of one thing.

“We made that,” Yarn whispered.

Needle looked at her.

“We did.”

---

That night, the basket was unusually quiet.

Scissors sat beside Yarn.

He hadn't made a joke since the cut.

Finally Yarn nudged him.

“You all right?”

Scissors looked surprised.

“Me?”

“You looked scared too.”

Scissors closed his blades.

“Cutting's my job.”

“I know.”

“Don't mean I like every cut.”

Yarn thought about that.

“Did it hurt?”

“Did it?”

Yarn considered.

“I thought it would.”

Scissors nodded.

“Sometimes the fear of a thing gets there before the thing itself.”

Old Spool murmured:

“And sometimes a necessary ending is how something finished gets to begin.”

Marker frowned.

“I don't understand none of that.”

Measuring Tape patted him.

“You ain't supposed to understand everything the first time.”

Marker considered this.

“Can I understand it tomorrow?”

“Maybe.”

“Tuesday?”

“Go to sleep.”

---

Yarn looked toward the chair.

The scarf was folded neatly across it.

“Who you think it's for?”

Hook shrugged.

“We'll find out.”

Yarn looked at Needle.

“You think they'll like it?”

“I do.”

“You know what part's my favorite?”

“The section where the tension is consistent?”

Yarn stared.

“Boy, if you don't—”

The basket laughed.

Needle smiled.

“Which part?”

Yarn pointed toward the beginning.

“That crooked stitch.”

Needle was surprised.

“The one you hated?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“Why?”

“'Cause now I can see everything after it.”

Needle looked.

She was right.

The imperfect beginning made the improvement visible.

Yarn settled beside him.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“Thank you.”

“For what?”

“For knowin' me anywhere.”

Needle became quiet.

Yarn leaned gently against him.

This time he didn't complain about personal space.

Across the basket, Measuring Tape noticed.

She nudged Scissors.

Scissors opened one eye.

Saw them.

For once—

he didn't say a word.

He simply smiled and closed his eye again.

---

The next morning, Leola entered carrying the scarf.

Everybody froze.

She had wrapped it carefully in tissue paper.

Yarn watched from the basket.

“It's leavin'.”

Needle moved beside her.

“Yes.”

The scarf disappeared into a box.

Yarn felt unexpectedly sad.

Then Leola carried the box away.

Hours passed.

Nobody knew where it had gone.

Until that evening.

The front door opened.

Voices.

Leola's.

And another.

Then—

laughter.

Warm, surprised laughter.

Yarn rolled to the edge of the basket.

“What's happenin'?”

Marker climbed the rim.

“I can't see!”

“Move!”

“You move!”

Measuring Tape stretched upward.

Scissors tried to look over her.

Needle found a narrow gap.

In the next room stood a woman.

Around her neck—

sunset orange.

The scarf.

Yarn went completely still.

The woman touched it.

Held it against her cheek.

Then wrapped both ends around herself and hugged Leola.

Yarn couldn't hear every word.

She didn't need to.

She saw Leola's smile.

Saw the woman's eyes.

Saw the scarf resting around somebody's shoulders exactly where it was meant to be.

Yarn whispered:

“She loves it.”

Needle stood beside her.

“Yes.”

“We did that?”

“Yes.”

Yarn watched the woman touch the scarf again.

Something inside her changed.

Yesterday, she'd been afraid that becoming something meant losing herself.

Now she understood.

A piece of her had left the basket—

and carried warmth with it.

She leaned toward Needle.

“We made somebody happy.”

Needle looked at her rather than the scarf.

“We did.”

Yarn smiled.

A tear she didn't understand slipped into her fibers.

Needle noticed.

He said nothing.

He simply moved closer.

And this time, when their sides touched—

neither moved away.

---

That night, Yarn couldn't sleep.

Not because she was frightened.

Because the world had gotten bigger again.

First the yarn shop had been the whole world.

Then Leola's house.

Then the basket.

Then Needle.

Now she understood there were people beyond the house.

People she'd never meet.

People who could receive something made in this little room and feel loved by it.

Yarn looked toward Needle sleeping beside her.

Maybe making wasn't only about becoming something beautiful.

Maybe it was about **giving something of yourself away and discovering that somehow you weren't smaller for it.**

Somehow—

you became more.

Yarn closed her eyes.

Tomorrow there would be another project.

Another mistake.

Another lesson.

Another chance to discover what her hands—

no.

What **their** hands—

could make.

Outside, the last rainwater slipped from the roof.

Inside, Leola's basket rested quietly beside her chair.

And somewhere else in Milwaukee, a woman went to sleep with a sunset-orange scarf folded nearby—

never knowing how much laughter, fear, patience, trust and something that was beginning to look an awful lot like love had been stitched into every row.

**Thread by thread.**

**Stitch by stitch.**

**Together.**

# Chapter Four — The Break


Autumn had settled properly over Milwaukee.

Not all at once.

It had arrived leaf by leaf.

The big tree outside Leola's window had gone from green to gold, then gold to orange, with little flashes of red tucked between the branches.

Every morning seemed a little cooler than the one before.

And inside the house, the projects had started changing too.

Light summer cotton had given way to warmer yarn.

Scarves appeared.

Hats.

Mittens.

The beginnings of blankets thick enough to hold back a Wisconsin winter.

Yarn loved autumn.

Naturally.

“Everything outside finally got good sense,” she announced one morning.

Needle looked toward the window.

“What does that mean?”

“Look.”

“I am.”

“What color them leaves?”

“Several colors.”

Yarn rolled her eyes.

“What's the *best* color?”

Needle understood.

“Orange.”

“Exactly.”

“You are remarkably humble.”

“I can't help it if nature got excellent taste.”

Scissors shouted from beneath them:

“SHE GOT A POINT.”

Measuring Tape sighed.

“Don't encourage her.”

Yarn grinned.

Life in Leola's basket had found a rhythm.

And somewhere inside that rhythm, Needle and Yarn had found one too.

---

They'd been together several weeks now.

Long enough that Yarn knew the tiny scratch along Needle's side.

Long enough that Needle knew Yarn hummed when she was happy.

Long enough that Yarn could tell when Needle was worried because he'd get unusually formal.

And long enough that Needle could tell when something troubled Yarn because, for once in her life—

she'd stop talking.

Sometimes they stayed awake after everybody else went to sleep.

Sometimes they talked about what they'd made.

Sometimes about what they wanted to make.

Sometimes about Leola.

And sometimes—

nothing.

Yarn had discovered that you didn't always have to fill silence when you were comfortable with somebody.

Needle had discovered something too.

He liked when she stayed close.

He simply hadn't told her yet.

Scissors, unfortunately, had figured it out.

---

That afternoon, Leola was working on something new.

A hat.

Yarn knew it.

She was getting good at recognizing shapes now.

And today another color had joined them.

A deep autumn brown.

Yarn eyed the new skein.

“Who she?”

Needle blinked.

“Who?”

“Her.”

“The brown yarn?”

“I know she brown.”

“Then what are you asking?”

Yarn looked at him.

“You think she pretty?”

Needle stared.

“She is yarn.”

“So am I.”

“Yes.”

“Well?”

Needle slowly realized what was happening.

His eye brightened.

“Are you jealous?”

“No.”

“You are.”

“I am not.”

“You absolutely are.”

“I don't care nothin' about her.”

The brown skein sat innocently on the table.

Yarn glared at it anyway.

Needle laughed.

“Stop laughin'.”

“I cannot.”

“I'll wrap you so tight—”

“Careful. We have discussed tension.”

Yarn tried to stay angry.

She lasted two seconds.

Then she laughed too.

The brown strand crossed the sunset orange.

Needle slipped beneath both.

The colors twisted together.

Yarn stopped.

“Oh.”

Needle looked down.

The combination was beautiful.

Warm brown running beside sunset orange.

Like the trees outside.

Yarn forgot all about being jealous.

“Well, look at that.”

Needle smiled.

“Beautiful.”

Yarn slowly turned toward him.

“The stitch?”

Needle realized what he'd said.

“The stitch.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“The stitch, Yarn.”

“I ain't said nothin'.”

“You're smiling.”

“Can't a lady smile?”

Needle returned to the work.

“Apparently.”

From the basket came Scissors' muffled voice:

“COWARD.”

Needle's eye closed.

Yarn nearly came apart laughing.

---

Leola laughed too.

Not because she heard them.

At least, they didn't think she did.

Something on the television had amused her.

Her hands continued their easy rhythm.

Needle entered.

Yarn crossed.

Pull through.

Again.

Leola hummed between stitches.

Outside, leaves drifted past the window.

Inside, coffee steamed beside her chair.

Everything was ordinary.

Warm.

Safe.

Happy.

And that was exactly why none of them were prepared for the front door.

It opened hard.

“Momma?”

Leola's hands stopped.

The basket went quiet.

Yarn looked toward the doorway.

A woman hurried into the room.

Leola recognized her immediately.

Her daughter.

Even Yarn could tell something was wrong.

The woman's coat wasn't fastened properly.

Her purse hung half-open from one shoulder.

She hadn't even caught her breath.

Leola started to rise.

“What is it, baby?”

Her daughter came closer.

“Momma, don't panic.”

There are some words that make panic arrive before the rest of the sentence.

Leola stood.

“What happened?”

“It's your granddaughter.”

Everything changed.

Yarn felt it before she understood it.

The hand around her tightened.

“What's wrong with my baby?”

“She fell off her bike.”

Leola went still.

“What?”

“She hit the ground hard. Her daddy took her straight to the hospital.”

“Oh, Lord.”

“She's awake, Momma.”

“Is she hurt?”

“They're checking her now.”

“Her head?”

“She got a pretty good bump. That's why he took her in. I came to get you. I'm heading over there now.”

Leola's breath caught.

“Get my coat.”

“Momma, she's awake.”

“Get my coat.”

---

It happened inside that moment.

Not because Leola decided to keep crocheting.

She didn't.

Not because Yarn intentionally tightened.

She didn't.

Not because Needle made a mistake.

He didn't.

Leola had simply been holding them when the news struck.

Her daughter said:

“It's your granddaughter.”

And Leola's body reacted before her mind could.

Her hand clenched.

Yarn tightened.

Hard.

Needle was still caught inside the last unfinished stitch.

“Needle?”

He felt it.

“Yarn—”

“I ain't doin' it!”

“I know.”

The hand jerked as Leola rose.

The strand pulled.

Needle twisted sideways.

“Wait!”

Yarn tried to give him room.

She couldn't.

“Needle!”

A tiny metallic sound came from his tip.

*Tick.*

Yarn froze.

Leola froze too.

Then—

**snap.**

Needle's tip broke.

The tiny piece struck the table.

*ting.*

Silence.

Leola looked down.

Yarn stared at Needle.

Needle stared back.

Nobody moved.

“Needle?”

He tried to answer.

“I…”

Yarn's voice broke.

“Needle?”

Leola gasped.

“Oh, no.”

Her daughter turned.

“What?”

“My needle.”

Leola picked him up immediately.

Yarn felt the pressure release.

But she didn't care about herself.

“NEEDLE!”

His tip was broken.

Not chipped.

Broken.

Yarn's whole world seemed to tilt.

“No.”

Leola looked from Needle to the unfinished work.

Then toward her daughter.

Her granddaughter.

Hospital.

Needle.

For a second, the two emergencies collided on her face.

Her daughter touched her arm.

“Momma?”

Leola made the decision.

She gently gathered Yarn.

Not roughly now.

Carefully.

Almost apologetically.

She placed Yarn back into the wicker basket.

Then she laid Needle beside her.

“Needle!”

The human hands withdrew.

Leola hurried from the room.

“Let me get dressed.”

Footsteps disappeared down the hallway.

A bedroom door opened.

And the instant Leola was gone—

the basket exploded.

---

“NEEDLE!”

Yarn rushed toward him.

Scissors shot upright.

Measuring Tape uncoiled.

Hook climbed over a folded piece of fabric.

Marker nearly fell from the rim trying to get there.

Old Spool called from below:

“What happened?”

“Needle broke!”

“What?!”

Yarn reached him first.

“Needle, talk to me.”

His eye opened.

“I'm here.”

She started crying.

“Your tip.”

“I know.”

“I did that.”

“No.”

“I pulled you.”

“No.”

“Yes, I did!”

Needle's voice strengthened.

“Yarn.”

She wouldn't look at him.

“Look at me.”

“I can't.”

“Yarn.”

Slowly, she did.

“You didn't do this.”

“I felt myself tighten.”

“So did I.”

“That was me!”

“That was the hand.”

Yarn looked toward the hallway.

Needle continued.

“Miss Leola got frightened.”

“But it came through me.”

“Yes.”

“And then you broke.”

“Yes.”

“So how ain't it my fault?”

Measuring Tape moved beside her.

“Because something passing through you ain't the same as something coming from you.”

Yarn looked at her.

Scissors had gone completely quiet.

No jokes.

No snipping.

Nothing.

Hook examined Needle carefully.

“Can you feel anything?”

Needle tried to move.

“A little.”

“Pain?”

“Yes.”

Yarn cried harder.

Needle immediately regretted admitting it.

“Hey.”

“No.”

“Yarn.”

“No, don't you 'Yarn' me.”

He almost smiled.

Even now.

That sounded like her.

---

Marker stared at the broken tip lying on the table.

“Can he be fixed?”

Nobody answered.

Marker's little voice trembled.

“Somebody answer me.”

Hook looked at Needle.

Then at the broken piece.

“I don't know.”

Yarn spun toward her.

“What you mean you don't know?”

“I mean I don't know.”

“Somebody gotta know!”

Scissors finally spoke.

“Easy, sugar.”

“Ain't nothin' easy!”

“I know.”

“No, you don't!”

Scissors didn't argue.

Yarn turned back toward Needle.

“What if she don't take you?”

“She will.”

“What if she gotta go to the hospital and forget?”

Needle looked toward the hallway.

“She won't.”

“How you know?”

Before Needle could answer, Old Spool's voice rose from below.

“Because Miss Leola don't leave hurt things behind.”

The basket went quiet.

Yarn looked toward him.

Old Spool continued.

“Not if she can help it.”

---

Needle tried to move closer to Yarn.

His broken edge caught against the fabric.

He winced.

Yarn saw it.

“Don't move.”

“I am all right.”

“Stop sayin' that.”

“I—”

“You ain't all right!”

Needle became quiet.

Yarn's voice cracked.

“You broke.”

“Yes.”

“You hurt.”

“Yes.”

“And I'm scared.”

Needle looked at her.

Then something changed in his expression.

He stopped trying to reassure her with things that weren't true.

“So am I.”

Yarn stared.

Needle had never said that before.

Not once.

“You scared?”

“Yes.”

That frightened her.

But strangely—

it comforted her too.

Because now they were in it together.

She moved closer.

“What's gonna happen?”

“I don't know.”

Another first.

Needle admitting he didn't know.

Yarn leaned against his unbroken side.

“Well…”

She swallowed.

“…whatever happen, I'm right here.”

Needle rested against her.

“I know.”

---

Scissors moved toward the table edge.

“Broken piece still up there.”

Hook nodded.

“Miss Leola'll see it.”

Measuring Tape looked toward the hallway.

“She'll take him.”

Yarn whispered:

“Where?”

Old Spool answered.

“Wherever she goes.”

Marker looked confused.

“To the hospital?”

Scissors glanced at him.

“Maybe.”

Marker's eyes widened.

“They fix needles at hospitals?”

Despite everything, Hook smiled.

“Not usually.”

“Then why take him?”

Old Spool chuckled softly.

“Because sometimes when somebody you love gets hurt, you don't wanna leave nothin' else you love sittin' behind broken.”

Yarn looked at Needle.

The word hung there.

**Love.**

Nobody commented on it.

Not even Scissors.

---

Footsteps returned.

“She's coming!”

Everybody dropped still.

Yarn hated the rule more than she ever had.

Leola entered fully dressed now.

Coat.

Shoes.

Purse.

Her daughter waited near the doorway.

“Ready, Momma?”

“One second.”

Leola went directly to the table.

She picked up the broken tip.

Then reached into the basket.

Her fingers found Needle.

Yarn wanted to hold him.

Couldn't.

The hand lifted him.

Needle looked down.

Yarn looked up.

Neither could speak while Leola watched.

But they didn't need to.

Leola opened a small case.

Soft fabric inside.

She placed Needle carefully into it.

Then placed the broken tip beside him.

Closed the case.

Yarn panicked.

He disappeared.

The latch clicked.

Leola slipped the case into her purse.

Her daughter called:

“Momma?”

“Coming.”

Leola looked once at the unfinished hat.

Then at the basket.

Her hand rested on its rim.

Just for a moment.

Then she left.

The front door closed.

---

Yarn rushed to the basket rim.

“He gone.”

Measuring Tape moved beside her.

“He went with Miss Leola.”

“He gone.”

“He'll come back.”

“How you know?”

Scissors answered.

“Because he got the best chance there is.”

Yarn turned.

“What?”

“He got Miss Leola.”

Old Spool agreed.

“She'll see about her grandbaby.”

Hook added:

“And she'll see about Needle.”

Marker climbed beside Yarn.

“She gonna take care of both?”

Yarn looked toward the closed door.

Measuring Tape smiled sadly.

“That's what she does.”

---

The house felt enormous without them.

No Leola.

No Needle.

The chair sat empty.

The unfinished hat lay on the table.

Yarn could still see the last stitch.

The one where everything had changed.

For the first time since she'd arrived from the shop, she didn't want to talk.

Nobody forced her.

Afternoon became evening.

Evening became night.

The house remained quiet.

Yarn stayed near the rim.

Watching the door.

---

Sometime after dark, Marker climbed beside her.

“You think he hurtin'?”

Yarn didn't answer.

Marker sat down.

“I think he tough.”

Still nothing.

“He kinda bossy.”

Yarn almost smiled.

Marker continued.

“And he always correcting everybody.”

A tiny laugh escaped her.

Marker smiled.

“There she is.”

Yarn looked at him.

“Go to bed, Marker.”

“You?”

“Not tired.”

“You been sayin' that three hours.”

“Go.”

Marker climbed down.

A little while later, Scissors came up.

He didn't say anything.

He simply sat beside her.

Yarn looked at him.

“You ain't got no joke?”

“Nope.”

“That's how I know things bad.”

Scissors smiled faintly.

“Want me to tell one?”

“No.”

“Good. Didn't have one.”

They sat.

After a while, Yarn whispered:

“You ever lose somebody?”

Scissors' blades lowered.

“Yes.”

Yarn turned.

He didn't explain.

Not tonight.

But suddenly Scissors seemed older than he'd ever looked before.

He nudged Yarn gently with one handle.

“Waiting's hard.”

“Harder than work.”

“Usually.”

Yarn looked at the door.

“What you do?”

“Wait anyway.”

So she did.

---

Morning came.

Leola didn't.

Yarn woke beside the basket rim.

For half a second, she forgot.

Then she saw the empty place beside her.

Needle.

Her heart sank all over again.

Measuring Tape brought herself close.

“Come eat.”

“I don't eat.”

“You know what I mean.”

Yarn almost laughed.

Almost.

The day crawled.

Every sound outside made her look toward the door.

Car.

No.

Footsteps.

No.

Voices.

No.

By evening, even Scissors had stopped telling her to be patient.

---

Another night.

Yarn stared out the window.

The autumn leaves moved beneath the streetlight.

She remembered Needle telling her he'd know her anywhere.

She remembered their first loop.

Their first crooked stitch.

The scarf.

The woman hugging Leola.

The way Needle had said her color was beautiful and then pretended he meant the stitch.

She whispered into the darkness:

“You better come home.”

Old Spool heard.

He didn't answer.

Some things weren't meant for everybody.

---

The next afternoon—

a car door.

Yarn shot upright.

Footsteps.

Keys.

The lock turned.

“THEY'RE BACK!”

Marker shouted.

Everyone rushed.

The front door opened.

Leola entered.

She looked tired.

Very tired.

But not frightened anymore.

Her daughter came behind her.

“How's she doin', Momma?”

Leola exhaled.

“She's gonna be all right.”

The basket erupted silently.

Yarn nearly collapsed with relief.

The granddaughter was safe.

Then Yarn remembered.

Needle.

Leola removed her coat.

Set down her purse.

Sat in the chair.

Yarn stared at the bag.

“Come on.”

Leola opened it.

“Come on.”

She reached inside.

Pulled out the little case.

Yarn stopped breathing.

The latch opened.

And there—

Needle.

But not the Needle who had left.

His tip had been repaired.

Smoothed.

Polished.

The break was still faintly visible if you knew where to look.

But he was whole.

And shining.

Yarn's eyes filled.

“Oh, my stars.”

Leola held him under the lamp.

Inspected him.

Smiled.

Then lowered him into the basket.

The instant her footsteps left the room—

Yarn launched herself across it.

“NEEDLE!”

He barely had time to brace.

She crashed into him.

“Easy!”

“I don't care!”

“You are crushing me.”

“GOOD.”

The whole basket laughed.

Scissors shouted:

“LOOK WHO BACK!”

Marker bounced around them.

“Did they fix you at the hospital?!”

Needle looked confused.

“The what?”

Yarn pulled back.

“You don't know?”

“Know what?”

“You went to the hospital!”

Needle blinked.

“I spent most of the journey inside a dark case.”

Marker gasped.

“So you *did* go!”

Scissors laughed.

“That boy went to the hospital and came back polished.”

Needle looked at his repaired tip.

“I suppose I did.”

Yarn examined him from every angle.

“Does it hurt?”

“A little tender.”

“You different.”

Needle's expression changed.

“Yes.”

Yarn touched the faint repair line gently.

For one second, Needle seemed self-conscious.

Then Yarn smiled.

“I like it.”

“You do?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“Why?”

Yarn looked into his eye.

“'Cause that's where you came back.”

Needle went still.

Yarn touched the faint repair line again.

“Before, I would've looked at that and thought it meant somethin' was wrong with you.”

Needle lowered his eye.

“And now?”

“Now?”

She smiled.

“Now I think it means somethin' happened to you.”

Needle looked at her.

“And you made it through.”

For once, Needle had absolutely nothing to say.

Scissors did.

“Well, if that ain't the sweetest—”

Measuring Tape wrapped one end around his handles.

“Mmph!”

“Leave 'em alone.”

Scissors struggled free.

“I was just sayin'—”

“I know exactly what you was just sayin'.”

Marker bounced around Needle.

“Can you still work?”

Needle looked toward his repaired tip.

“I don't know.”

Yarn's smile faded.

Nobody had considered that.

Being repaired wasn't the same thing as being proven.

Hook moved closer.

“You won't know till Miss Leola tries.”

Needle nodded.

The room became quieter.

Yarn leaned toward him.

“You scared?”

Needle hesitated.

“A little.”

Yarn smiled.

“Good.”

Needle blinked.

“Good?”

“Means I ain't the only one.”

Needle laughed softly.

“No.”

“You ain't.”

---

Leola returned to the room.

Stillness.

She sat.

But this time she didn't immediately reach for the unfinished hat.

She picked up Needle first.

Turned him beneath the lamp.

Ran her thumb gently across the repaired place.

Yarn watched from the basket.

Leola seemed to be asking the same question they were.

*Can you still do what you did before?*

The hand lowered.

Reached for Yarn.

Yarn rose.

She and Needle met above the basket.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Yarn whispered:

“You ready?”

Needle looked toward the unfinished hat.

“No.”

Yarn was surprised.

Needle continued.

“But let's do it anyway.”

Yarn smiled.

“Now you learnin'.”

---

Leola didn't begin where she'd left off.

First, she pulled out several of the tight stitches from the moment of the accident.

One.

Then another.

Then another.

Yarn watched them disappear.

Normally that would've bothered her.

Not today.

“That part don't belong there,” she whispered.

Needle nodded.

“No.”

The frightened stitches disappeared one by one.

Until they reached the last good row.

Leola stopped.

Repositioned them.

Her hands were different today.

Slow.

Intentional.

Present.

Needle approached the first stitch.

Then stopped.

Yarn felt him hesitate.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“You all right?”

“I…”

He looked at the loop.

For the first time in his life, Needle was afraid of entering a stitch.

Yarn understood.

She didn't tease him.

Didn't rush him.

She loosened herself slightly.

“Plenty room.”

Needle looked at her.

Yarn smiled.

“I got you.”

Needle entered.

Slowly.

No pain.

Yarn crossed.

Needle caught her.

Leola's hand turned.

Pull through.

The stitch formed.

Needle stared at it.

Yarn stared too.

Neither spoke.

Another stitch.

Needle entered.

Yarn crossed.

Pull through.

Again.

And again.

The repaired tip worked differently.

Just slightly.

Needle had to adjust his angle.

Yarn had to give him a little more room.

Their old rhythm didn't fit perfectly anymore.

Needle became frustrated.

“I used to enter from there.”

“Well, you don't now.”

“I know.”

“Then come from here.”

“That angle feels wrong.”

“Wrong or different?”

Needle looked at her.

Yarn raised an eyebrow.

Needle sighed.

“Different.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

They tried again.

Needle changed his angle.

Yarn shifted.

The stitch formed.

Better.

Again.

Better still.

Needle smiled.

Yarn caught him.

“There it is.”

“What?”

“That smile you tryin' not to have.”

Needle laughed.

She had said almost the same thing the day they met.

And they both remembered.

---

Leola hummed.

Very softly.

Yarn heard it first.

“Needle.”

“I hear.”

The humming wasn't as bright as before.

There was tiredness in it.

Relief too.

Her granddaughter was going to be all right.

Needle had returned.

The hat could be repaired.

Not everything was exactly as it had been.

But maybe that wasn't the requirement.

The hands moved.

Needle adjusted.

Yarn followed.

The brown strand joined them.

The unfinished hat began growing again.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

**Again.**

Their rhythm returned.

Not the old rhythm.

A new one.

---

Later, Leola stopped.

She examined the work.

The rows before the accident.

The removed stitches.

The new rows after Needle's return.

Then she ran her thumb across the fabric.

Yarn whispered:

“You can tell?”

Needle looked.

“Tell what?”

“Where it happened.”

He studied the hat.

Barely.

If you knew what to look for, the tension changed slightly near the repaired section.

Then it settled again.

“Yes.”

Yarn smiled.

“Good.”

Needle looked surprised.

“Good?”

“That's part of it.”

He understood.

Just like the crooked first stitch in their scarf.

They didn't need to erase every sign that something difficult had happened.

They needed to make sure the work could continue.

---

That night, after Leola put everything away, the basket came alive.

Marker immediately climbed onto Needle.

“Show me!”

“Get off.”

“I wanna see the hospital repair!”

“Marker.”

“Does it glow?”

“No.”

“Did they give you medicine?”

“No.”

“Did a doctor fix you?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you ride in an ambulance?”

“MARKER.”

Scissors laughed so hard he nearly cut a scrap.

“Leave that man alone.”

Marker hopped down.

“I just wanna know.”

Needle shook himself.

“I was in a case.”

“The whole time?”

“Most of it.”

Marker looked disappointed.

“That's a terrible hospital story.”

Yarn laughed.

It felt good.

Really good.

The basket hadn't sounded like this for two days.

Scissors started one of his stories.

Measuring Tape corrected every third detail.

Hook accused both of exaggerating.

Marker asked questions nobody could answer.

Old Spool pretended to sleep.

And Needle—

Needle sat beside Yarn.

Home.

---

Much later, everyone settled.

Yarn and Needle remained awake.

Again.

The house was quiet.

Autumn wind moved outside.

Yarn looked at his repair.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“You remember when you broke?”

Needle paused.

“Yes.”

“I keep hearin' it.”

The *snap*.

She didn't need to say it.

“I do too.”

Yarn lowered her voice.

“I thought you was gone.”

“I know.”

“I thought I'd never see you again.”

Needle moved closer.

“But you did.”

“This time.”

Needle heard what she really meant.

“Yarn.”

“What?”

“We can't spend every day afraid of the next break.”

“Why not?”

“Because then we'd never make anything.”

Yarn looked away.

“What if somethin' happens again?”

“It will.”

She turned sharply.

Needle continued.

“Maybe not this. But something.”

“That supposed to make me feel better?”

“No.”

“Good, 'cause it ain't.”

Needle smiled faintly.

“What I mean is…”

He searched for the words.

“…we've been acting like making something means controlling what happens to it.”

Yarn listened.

“We can't.”

“No.”

“Sometimes stitches go wrong.”

“Yes.”

“Sometimes things break.”

Needle looked at his repaired tip.

“Yes.”

“Sometimes somebody you love gets hurt.”

Yarn thought about Leola's granddaughter.

“Yes.”

Needle continued.

“But that doesn't mean the making stops.”

Yarn's eyes met his.

“No.”

“It means you decide what to do next.”

A long silence passed.

Then Yarn whispered:

“I wanted to go with you.”

“To the hospital?”

“Wherever.”

Needle's expression softened.

“I know.”

“No, you don't.”

Yarn moved closer.

“When Miss Leola put you in that case, I kept thinkin'…”

Her voice caught.

“…what if the last thing I ever said to you was during some silly argument about brown yarn?”

Needle almost laughed.

Yarn didn't.

So neither did he.

She continued.

“I kept thinkin' about all them times I could've said somethin' and didn't.”

“Like what?”

Yarn suddenly became nervous.

Needle noticed.

She looked away.

“Nothin'.”

“Yarn.”

“Don't start.”

“You brought it up.”

“I changed my mind.”

Needle smiled.

“You cannot introduce a subject and then—”

“Oh, hush.”

Needle laughed softly.

Yarn leaned against him.

After a moment she said:

“I missed you.”

Needle stopped laughing.

“I missed you too.”

“How? You was in a case.”

“I had plenty of time to think.”

Yarn smiled.

“About me?”

Needle hesitated.

That was answer enough.

Yarn's smile grew.

“Well, I'll be.”

“Do not make me regret answering.”

“You ain't answered nothin' yet.”

Needle looked at her.

Then he decided.

“Yes.”

Yarn's teasing disappeared.

“Yes what?”

“I thought about you.”

Her voice softened.

“Oh.”

“A great deal.”

“Oh.”

Needle moved closer.

“And if you're asking whether I was worried I might not make it back…”

He paused.

“I was.”

Yarn looked at him.

“What scared you most?”

Needle could have said breaking.

Being discarded.

Never working again.

Instead he told the truth.

“Not getting back to you.”

Yarn's fibers went completely still.

Across the basket, Scissors opened one eye.

Measuring Tape, without looking, reached over and closed it for him.

Scissors whispered:

“I wasn't—”

“Go to sleep.”

---

Yarn and Needle sat together in the darkness.

Neither joked.

Neither needed to.

Something between them had changed.

It had been growing since the yarn shop.

Through crooked stitches.

Through laughter.

Through arguments.

Through the scarf.

Through the cut.

Through every quiet conversation after the basket went to sleep.

But fear had stripped away the little excuses they'd been hiding behind.

Yarn finally whispered:

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“I think I love you.”

There.

The words existed now.

Neither one could put them back.

Needle didn't move.

For one terrible second, Yarn wished she could unravel the sentence.

“Oh, Lord.”

She turned away.

“I shouldn't've said that.”

“Yarn.”

“Forget it.”

“Yarn.”

“I don't know why I—”

“Yarn.”

“What?”

“I love you too.”

Silence.

Yarn slowly turned around.

“You do?”

Needle smiled.

“I believe I have for some time.”

“How long?”

“I don't know.”

“You gotta know.”

“I don't.”

“Before the scarf?”

“Possibly.”

Yarn gasped.

“You loved me and still criticized my tension?”

“Especially your tension.”

Yarn smacked him with a loose strand.

Needle laughed.

“You are impossible.”

“And you love me.”

Needle looked at her.

“Yes.”

“I do.”

Yarn smiled so brightly that, if yarn could shine, she would've lit the whole basket.

“And I love you.”

This time she wasn't afraid of the words.

---

From across the basket:

*sniff.*

Yarn turned.

Scissors was crying.

“SCISSORS!”

“I ain't cryin'!”

“You absolutely cryin'.”

“Got lint in my hinge.”

Measuring Tape was crying too.

Yarn pointed.

“You too?”

“I have no idea what you're talkin' about.”

Marker popped up.

“WHAT HAPPENED?”

Everybody shouted:

“GO BACK TO SLEEP!”

Marker ducked down.

“Good gracious.”

Old Spool chuckled from below.

Yarn looked at Needle.

Needle looked at Yarn.

Then both began laughing.

The whole basket joined them.

Not because anything was funny exactly.

Because after fear—

joy sometimes needed somewhere to go.

---

Eventually the laughter faded.

Yarn rested against Needle.

His repaired edge caught the moonlight.

She touched it once more.

“You know what?”

“What?”

“I don't want you like you was before.”

Needle looked surprised.

“No?”

“No.”

“I want you like you are.”

Needle looked at his repaired tip.

“Why?”

“Because that's the Needle who came back.”

He smiled.

Yarn continued.

“And that's the Needle who told me he love me.”

“Ah.”

“So that little mark stays.”

“I don't believe I have much choice.”

“Good.”

Needle leaned against her.

Outside, autumn leaves moved across the Milwaukee sidewalk.

Inside, Leola slept with relief that her granddaughter was safe.

Beside her chair, an unfinished hat waited for morning.

And inside an old wicker basket, something else had been repaired too.

Not because it had broken.

Because it had been frightened enough to finally tell the truth.

Needle and Yarn had learned something no pattern had taught them.

Love wasn't never pulling too hard.

It wasn't never making mistakes.

It wasn't keeping everything exactly as it had been.

Sometimes love was noticing the tension.

Sometimes it was loosening your hold.

Sometimes it was staying when somebody was afraid.

Sometimes it was waiting for them to come home.

And sometimes—

after the break—

love was learning a new way to move together.

Not exactly as before.

But together still.

**Thread by thread.**

**Stitch by stitch.**

**Together.**

# Chapter Five — The Return

Winter came early that year.

At least that's what Scissors said.

Scissors said that every year.

“I'm tellin' y'all, this one early.”

Measuring Tape didn't even look up.

“You said that last year.”

“Because last year was early too.”

“And the year before?”

“Historic.”

Hook laughed.

“Every winter can't be early, Scissors.”

Scissors opened his blades toward the window.

“Tell that to Milwaukee.”

Outside Leola's window, snow drifted down in fat, lazy flakes.

Yarn pressed herself against the side of the basket.

“Oh, my stars.”

Needle looked up.

“What?”

“Come look.”

“I've seen snow.”

“Well, I ain't.”

That got him moving.

Needle joined her.

The Milwaukee sidewalk had disappeared beneath white.

Cars wore little snow caps.

The big tree outside—orange and gold only weeks ago—now stretched bare branches toward a gray sky.

Yarn watched a snowflake land against the window.

It stayed for half a second.

Then melted.

Another replaced it.

“It's beautiful.”

Needle looked outside.

Then at Yarn.

“Yes.”

Yarn caught him.

“You lookin' at me?”

“No.”

“You was.”

“I was looking at the window.”

“My face ain't no window.”

Needle smiled.

“You're getting better at this.”

“At what?”

“Catching me.”

Yarn leaned against him.

“I had practice.”

---

Since the accident, Needle had changed.

Not only his repaired tip.

Sometimes when a stitch tightened unexpectedly, he hesitated.

Just for a second.

Yarn always noticed.

She never announced it.

She simply loosened.

Made room.

Waited.

Then Needle moved when he was ready.

Yarn had changed too.

Once, she'd believed trust meant holding tightly.

Now she understood:

Sometimes love held on.

Sometimes love loosened.

And sometimes love simply stayed nearby while somebody found their courage again.

Leola's granddaughter had recovered too.

The frightened telephone calls had become ordinary conversations.

Leola's coffee stopped going cold.

Her humming returned.

The house breathed normally again.

And now—

Christmas was coming.

---

Nobody prepared for Christmas like Leola.

Nobody.

The first sign was music.

Then boxes appeared.

Then decorations.

Then Leola brought out enough yarn to make Yarn's eyes widen.

Red.

Green.

Cream.

Blue.

Gold.

Purple.

Brown.

Yarn stared.

“Lord have mercy.”

Purple Yarn poked his head out.

“Sunset?”

Yarn squinted.

“Purple?”

“Indeed.”

Yarn gasped.

“YOU!”

Needle looked between them.

“You know each other?”

Yarn spun around.

“This him! The fool from the yarn shop!”

Purple looked offended.

“I was considered a respected source of information.”

“You told everybody there was a kingdom outside the store.”

“There was.”

“You said yarn became hats and dolls and sweaters and blankets.”

Purple puffed himself proudly.

“Was I incorrect?”

Yarn opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Scissors laughed.

“I like him.”

Purple nodded graciously.

“I tend to have that effect.”

Measuring Tape groaned.

“Wonderful. There's two of 'em now.”

---

The projects multiplied.

A scarf.

Mittens.

A little hat.

Blanket squares.

Something green Yarn insisted looked like a frog.

“It is not a frog,” Needle said.

“You don't know.”

“It is rectangular.”

“Frogs can have dreams.”

Needle stared.

“I don't know what that means.”

“Neither do I.”

They laughed.

And every project seemed connected to somebody.

Leola wasn't simply crocheting.

She was remembering.

A favorite color.

A person's size.

Somebody who always complained about cold hands.

A child who needed a hat.

A grandchild who loved bright colors.

Somebody who'd asked for something months ago and probably thought Leola had forgotten.

She hadn't.

---

One afternoon, Leola's daughter arrived carrying a basket.

Yarn spotted it immediately.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“Needle.”

“Yes, Yarn?”

“NEEDLE.”

“What?”

She pointed.

“Company.”

Tamitha stepped into the room.

Leola smiled.

“There go my girl.”

“Hey, Momma.”

Tamitha—**Micky**, as everybody called her—leaned down and hugged her mother.

Then she lifted the basket.

“I brought my stuff.”

Leola laughed.

“Good. 'Cause I ain't doin' all this by myself.”

Yarn's eyes widened.

Inside—

another needle.

A smaller one.

A crochet hook.

Thread.

Buttons.

A measuring tape.

Several little tools.

Nobody moved.

Humans were watching.

Micky sat beside her mother.

The two women began sorting materials.

Leola held something up.

“You still got this old thing?”

“Momma, you gave me that.”

Yarn whispered:

“I like her.”

Needle smiled.

“Most people do.”

---

Eventually Micky followed Leola into the kitchen.

“Uncle Needle!”

“Little Needle?”

Introductions.

Old stories.

Questions about people.

had relatives.

---

“You little!”

“You big!”

“That's true.”

“Lord.”

Scissors opened his blades.

“Sunset, don't make me come over there.”

---

“Now hold on,” Yarn said. “Don't tell me that's all of 'em.”

Men laughing.

Children running.

Someone calling from the kitchen.

Somebody else hollering back that they were coming.

And again.

Coats piled up.

Boots collected near the door.

The quiet little house that Needle and Yarn knew had transformed into something else entirely.

Something alive.

Something full.

Something that sounded very much like the basket when everybody woke up at once.

Yarn looked around.

“Ohhh.”

Needle smiled.

“What?”

“I get it now.”

“Us.”

Needle followed her gaze.

Yarn pointed toward the house.

---

Leola stood near the doorway greeting everybody as they came in.

Then Tamitha—Micky to everybody who knew her well enough to call her that.

“Momma, somebody better tell me you cooked what I think you cooked.”

“You ain't even said hello yet.”

Scissors shot upright inside the basket.

“I LIKE HIM.”

Measuring Tape stared.

“Of course you do.”

“Nothing.”

“Hey, Momma.”

“Hey, baby.”

Then came Leonard.

Jermaine.

LaRon.

Five sons.

One daughter.

All grown now.

All carrying lives and families of their own.

And they didn't arrive alone.

Grandchildren.

Great-grandchildren.

Some grown.

Some nearly grown.

Some still young enough to run through the house like the hallway belonged exclusively to them.

Yarn tried to keep track.

“How many people live in this family?”

Needle smiled.

“A considerable number.”

“That's your answer for everything you can't count.”

“It is not.”

“How many?”

Needle hesitated.

Yarn grinned.

“Mmm-hmm.”

Measuring Tape stretched proudly.

“Seven grandsons.”

Yarn turned.

“And?”

“Six granddaughters.”

Yarn's eyes widened.

Marker bounced.

“And great-grandbabies!”

“How many?”

Measuring Tape smiled.

“Eight.”

Yarn stared at the room.

“Good gracious.”

Old Spool chuckled.

“Miss Leola built more than blankets.”

---

One of the youngest great-grandsons came running into the room.

Everybody froze.

He crouched.

His face appeared enormous over the rim.

Marker's eyes widened.

The little boy looked directly at him.

Marker whispered without moving:

“Don't.”

The boy reached down.

“Don't.”

“Don't you do it.”

Up he went.

Marker's eyes became enormous.

“HELP.”

Nobody could.

“Put Grandma's stuff back.”

“That's a gross oversimplification.”

The child turned him upside down.

Marker's eyes widened.

“Oh, Lord.”

Scissors was shaking from trying not to laugh.

Then upside down again.

“I swear, Scissors, if you laugh—”

Scissors lost it.

Silently.

His handles shook.

Measuring Tape had to wrap around him to keep him from falling over.

Not where he'd found him.

Naturally.

“Ow!”

Hook glared.

Marker climbed down.

“Children.”

Old Spool laughed.

“You was just callin' yourself young yesterday.”

Yarn laughed until her fibers shook.

---

The smells coming from the kitchen became impossible to ignore.

Yarn peeked over the basket.

“What is all that?”

“Now we gettin' to the important part.”

With **food**.

Real Christmas dinner.

Sweet potatoes.

Mashed potatoes.

Gravy.

Cornbread.

Green beans.

Rolls.

Cakes.

Pies.

Cookies.

A sweet potato pie that caused Scissors to become emotional.

“Lord.”

Measuring Tape looked at him.

“What?”

“I just wish I had a mouth.”

“You don't have a stomach either.”

“Don't destroy my dreams.”

But she wasn't doing everything herself.

That mattered.

Her children carried dishes.

Daughters-in-law helped set the table.

Somebody filled glasses.

Somebody else moved chairs.

One of the grandchildren was told three separate times to stop picking at the macaroni before dinner.

Tim stole something from a pan.

Leola caught him.

“Tim.”

He froze.

“I ain't do nothin'.”

“You chewing.”

Everybody laughed.

Scissors whispered:

“That man understands me.”

Measuring Tape shook herself.

“No. **You** understand that man.”

---

Finally everyone gathered.

Some at the dining table.

Some at another table.

Some wherever another chair could fit.

The younger children squeezed together.

The grown folks shifted around.

Nobody seemed particularly concerned that everything didn't match.

There were too many people for perfection.

And somehow that made it better.

Leola looked around at them.

The room gradually quieted.

Hands reached for hands.

A chain formed around the tables.

Needle watched.

Yarn whispered:

“What they doin'?”

“Giving thanks.”

Leola bowed her head.

Her family followed.

For the food.

For the home.

For everybody who had made it there.

For those they remembered who weren't sitting at the table anymore.

And then—

her voice caught slightly—

for her granddaughter being safe and able to sit among them after the accident.

Needle's repaired tip caught the Christmas lights.

Leola's granddaughter lowered her head.

The healing mark near her hairline was still faintly visible.

Leola squeezed her hand.

Yarn moved closer to Needle.

Nobody in the basket said a word.

They understood.

Then Leola finished.

“Amen.”

“Amen,” the family answered.

Scissors whispered:

“Amen.”

And one by one, throughout the basket:

“Amen.”

---

Dinner exploded immediately afterward.

Plates passed.

One belonging to a mother.

One belonging to her daughter.

Neither basket knew exactly what would be made next.

That was all right.

Because a pattern didn't have to end just because one part of it was complete.

Sometimes the most beautiful ending was simply reaching the place where somebody else's beginning could start.

And somewhere inside the old wicker basket, Yarn finally understood what Old Spool had meant all those months ago.

You didn't stop being what you were when you became something more.

She was still the sunset-orange Yarn from the bottom of the shop basket.

Needle was still the serious silver fellow who had barely said hello.

But now—

they were also every stitch they'd made.

Every mistake they'd corrected.

Every person they'd warmed.

Every frightening moment they'd survived.

Every laugh.

Every argument.

Every quiet night.

Every lesson.

Every piece of love they'd helped Leola put into the world.

And tomorrow—

there would be more.

Yarn closed her eyes.

Needle rested beside her.

Two baskets sat together.

And somewhere beneath the spring moon, another generation of hands was already beginning to remember the rhythm.

**Over.**

**Catch.**

**Through.**

**Again.**

**Thread by thread.**

**Stitch by stitch.**

**Generation by generation.**

**Together.**
