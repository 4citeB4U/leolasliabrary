# Chapter Five — The Return

Winter came early that year.

At least that's what Scissors said.

Scissors said that every year.

“I'm tellin' y'all, this one early.”

Measuring Tape didn't even look up.

“You said that last year.”

“Because last year was early too.”

“And the year before?”

“Historic.”

Hook laughed.

“Every winter can't be early, Scissors.”

Scissors opened his blades toward the window.

“Tell that to Milwaukee.”

Outside Leola's window, snow drifted down in fat, lazy flakes.

Yarn pressed herself against the side of the basket.

“Oh, my stars.”

Needle looked up.

“What?”

“Come look.”

“I've seen snow.”

“Well, I ain't.”

That got him moving.

Needle joined her.

The Milwaukee sidewalk had disappeared beneath white.

Cars wore little snow caps.

The big tree outside—orange and gold only weeks ago—now stretched bare branches toward a gray sky.

Yarn watched a snowflake land against the window.

It stayed for half a second.

Then melted.

Another replaced it.

“It's beautiful.”

Needle looked outside.

Then at Yarn.

“Yes.”

Yarn caught him.

“You lookin' at me?”

“No.”

“You was.”

“I was looking at the window.”

“My face ain't no window.”

Needle smiled.

“You're getting better at this.”

“At what?”

“Catching me.”

Yarn leaned against him.

“I had practice.”

---

Since the accident, Needle had changed.

Not only his repaired tip.

Sometimes when a stitch tightened unexpectedly, he hesitated.

Just for a second.

Yarn always noticed.

She never announced it.

She simply loosened.

Made room.

Waited.

Then Needle moved when he was ready.

Yarn had changed too.

Once, she'd believed trust meant holding tightly.

Now she understood:

Sometimes love held on.

Sometimes love loosened.

And sometimes love simply stayed nearby while somebody found their courage again.

Leola's granddaughter had recovered too.

The frightened telephone calls had become ordinary conversations.

Leola's coffee stopped going cold.

Her humming returned.

The house breathed normally again.

And now—

Christmas was coming.

---

Nobody prepared for Christmas like Leola.

Nobody.

The first sign was music.

Then boxes appeared.

Then decorations.

Then Leola brought out enough yarn to make Yarn's eyes widen.

Red.

Green.

Cream.

Blue.

Gold.

Purple.

Brown.

Yarn stared.

“Lord have mercy.”

Purple Yarn poked his head out.

“Sunset?”

Yarn squinted.

“Purple?”

“Indeed.”

Yarn gasped.

“YOU!”

Needle looked between them.

“You know each other?”

Yarn spun around.

“This him! The fool from the yarn shop!”

Purple looked offended.

“I was considered a respected source of information.”

“You told everybody there was a kingdom outside the store.”

“There was.”

“You said yarn became hats and dolls and sweaters and blankets.”

Purple puffed himself proudly.

“Was I incorrect?”

Yarn opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Scissors laughed.

“I like him.”

Purple nodded graciously.

“I tend to have that effect.”

Measuring Tape groaned.

“Wonderful. There's two of 'em now.”

---

The projects multiplied.

A scarf.

Mittens.

A little hat.

Blanket squares.

Something green Yarn insisted looked like a frog.

“It is not a frog,” Needle said.

“You don't know.”

“It is rectangular.”

“Frogs can have dreams.”

Needle stared.

“I don't know what that means.”

“Neither do I.”

They laughed.

And every project seemed connected to somebody.

Leola wasn't simply crocheting.

She was remembering.

A favorite color.

A person's size.

Somebody who always complained about cold hands.

A child who needed a hat.

A grandchild who loved bright colors.

Somebody who'd asked for something months ago and probably thought Leola had forgotten.

She hadn't.

---

One afternoon, Leola's daughter arrived carrying a basket.

Yarn spotted it immediately.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“Needle.”

“Yes, Yarn?”

“NEEDLE.”

“What?”

She pointed.

“Company.”

Tamitha stepped into the room.

Leola smiled.

“There go my girl.”

“Hey, Momma.”

Tamitha—**Micky**, as everybody called her—leaned down and hugged her mother.

Then she lifted the basket.

“I brought my stuff.”

Leola laughed.

“Good. 'Cause I ain't doin' all this by myself.”

Micky set her craft basket near Leola's.

Yarn's eyes widened.

Inside—

another needle.

A smaller one.

A crochet hook.

Thread.

Buttons.

A measuring tape.

Several little tools.

Nobody moved.

Humans were watching.

Micky sat beside her mother.

The two women began sorting materials.

Leola held something up.

“You still got this old thing?”

Micky laughed.

“Momma, you gave me that.”

“That's why I'm asking why you ain't took better care of it.”

“See? That's exactly why I almost left it home.”

Both women laughed.

Yarn whispered:

“I like her.”

Needle smiled.

“Most people do.”

---

Eventually Micky followed Leola into the kitchen.

The instant they disappeared—

both baskets came alive.

The little needle popped upright.

“Uncle Needle!”

Needle's eye widened.

“Little Needle?”

Yarn turned.

“UNCLE?”

Little Needle launched himself across the gap between the baskets.

Needle barely caught him.

“Look at you!”

“I got sharpened!”

“I can see that.”

“And I worked on three projects!”

“Three?”

“Three!”

Yarn stared.

Needle looked toward her.

“What?”

“You got family you ain't told me about?”

Needle hesitated.

“I may have neglected certain details.”

Scissors shouted:

“MAN BEEN HIDIN' RELATIVES.”

“I was not hiding anybody.”

A smaller pair of craft scissors climbed from Micky's basket.

“Still serious, ain't he?”

Scissors turned.

Then froze.

“Well, well, well.”

Little Scissors opened her blades.

“Don't start.”

“I ain't said nothin'.”

“You was about to.”

Scissors grinned.

“Family know family.”

Soon both baskets were talking at once.

Introductions.

Old stories.

Arguments about tools.

Arguments about who remembered the stories correctly.

Questions about projects.

Questions about people.

Yarn looked around in amazement.

Their little basket family—

had relatives.

---

A tiny plastic marker climbed out of Micky's basket.

Marker gasped.

“You little!”

The tiny marker bounced.

“You big!”

“I ain't big.”

“You bigger than me.”

Marker considered that.

“That's true.”

Within seconds they were climbing the basket walls.

“Don't fall,” Measuring Tape warned.

“We ain't!”

Both immediately fell.

Scissors caught them.

“Lord.”

Yarn laughed.

“That little one remind me of somebody.”

Measuring Tape smiled.

“Some personalities run through families.”

Yarn looked toward the kitchen.

“You mean the humans?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

Scissors stretched proudly.

“Take me.”

Measuring Tape groaned.

“Do we have to?”

“I got my sense of humor from Tim.”

Yarn blinked.

“Tim?”

“Miss Leola's boy.”

Scissors puffed himself up.

“Third-oldest.”

Needle corrected:

“You did not literally receive your personality from Tim.”

Scissors shrugged.

“Spend enough years listenin' to that man joke, eventually some of it sticks.”

Measuring Tape nodded.

“That's true.”

Scissors grinned.

“Tim walk into a room, somebody gon' laugh.”

Yarn looked at Scissors.

“So that's why you like this?”

“Like what?”

“Annoyin'.”

Everybody laughed.

Scissors opened his blades.

“Sunset, don't make me come over there.”

---

“Now hold on,” Yarn said. “Don't tell me that's all of 'em.”

Measuring Tape laughed.

“Oh, honey. Not even close.”

More voices poured in from the front of the house.

Men laughing.

Children running.

Someone calling from the kitchen.

Somebody else hollering back that they were coming.

The front door opened again.

And again.

Coats piled up.

Boots collected near the door.

The quiet little house that Needle and Yarn knew had transformed into something else entirely.

Something alive.

Something full.

Something that sounded very much like the basket when everybody woke up at once.

Yarn looked around.

“Ohhh.”

Needle smiled.

“What?”

“I get it now.”

“Get what?”

“Us.”

Needle followed her gaze.

Yarn pointed toward the house.

“We like them.”

---

Leola stood near the doorway greeting everybody as they came in.

She hugged Freddy.

Then Tamitha—Micky to everybody who knew her well enough to call her that.

Tim came through talking before he'd even gotten his coat off.

“Momma, somebody better tell me you cooked what I think you cooked.”

Leola laughed.

“You ain't even said hello yet.”

“I smelled hello from the driveway.”

The room erupted.

Scissors shot upright inside the basket.

“I LIKE HIM.”

Measuring Tape stared.

“Of course you do.”

“What?”

“Nothing.”

Tim hugged Leola.

“Hey, Momma.”

“Hey, baby.”

Then came Leonard.

Jermaine.

LaRon.

One by one, Leola's grown children filled the house.

Five sons.

One daughter.

All grown now.

All carrying lives and families of their own.

And they didn't arrive alone.

Spouses came behind them.

Grandchildren.

Great-grandchildren.

Some grown.

Some nearly grown.

Some still young enough to run through the house like the hallway belonged exclusively to them.

Yarn tried to keep track.

“How many people live in this family?”

Needle smiled.

“A considerable number.”

“That's your answer for everything you can't count.”

“It is not.”

“How many?”

Needle hesitated.

Yarn grinned.

“Mmm-hmm.”

Measuring Tape stretched proudly.

“Seven grandsons.”

Yarn turned.

“And?”

“Six granddaughters.”

Yarn's eyes widened.

Marker bounced.

“And great-grandbabies!”

“How many?”

Measuring Tape smiled.

“Eight.”

Yarn stared at the room.

“Good gracious.”

Old Spool chuckled.

“Miss Leola built more than blankets.”

---

One of the youngest great-grandsons came running into the room.

He stopped beside Leola's basket.

Everybody froze.

He crouched.

His face appeared enormous over the rim.

Marker's eyes widened.

The little boy looked directly at him.

Marker whispered without moving:

“Don't.”

The boy reached down.

“Don't.”

A finger came closer.

“Don't you do it.”

The finger hooked Marker.

Up he went.

Marker's eyes became enormous.

“HELP.”

Nobody moved.

Nobody could.

The little boy held Marker up.

“What is this?”

One of the adults called from across the room:

“Put Grandma's stuff back.”

“But what is it?”

“A stitch marker.”

“What it do?”

“It marks stitches.”

Marker looked offended.

“That's a gross oversimplification.”

The child turned him upside down.

Marker's eyes widened.

“Oh, Lord.”

Scissors was shaking from trying not to laugh.

The boy turned Marker sideways.

Then upside down again.

Marker whispered:

“I swear, Scissors, if you laugh—”

Scissors lost it.

Silently.

His handles shook.

Measuring Tape had to wrap around him to keep him from falling over.

Finally the boy placed Marker back.

Not where he'd found him.

Naturally.

Marker landed on top of Hook.

“Ow!”

“Sorry!”

Hook glared.

Marker climbed down.

“Children.”

Old Spool laughed.

“You was just callin' yourself young yesterday.”

“I have matured.”

“You got held upside down for thirty seconds.”

“It changed me.”

Yarn laughed until her fibers shook.

---

The smells coming from the kitchen became impossible to ignore.

Yarn peeked over the basket.

“What is all that?”

Scissors inhaled dramatically.

“Now we gettin' to the important part.”

The dining table was filling.

Not with one little plate.

With **food**.

Real Christmas dinner.

A glazed ham sat near the center.

Beside it were pans of dressing and macaroni and cheese, the top baked golden brown.

There were greens cooked down tender.

Sweet potatoes.

Mashed potatoes.

Gravy.

Cornbread.

Green beans.

Rolls.

And dishes that seemed to keep appearing every time Yarn looked away.

Then came desserts.

Cakes.

Pies.

Cookies.

A sweet potato pie that caused Scissors to become emotional.

“Lord.”

Measuring Tape looked at him.

“What?”

“I just wish I had a mouth.”

“You don't have a stomach either.”

“Don't destroy my dreams.”

Yarn watched Leola moving between the kitchen and dining room.

But she wasn't doing everything herself.

That mattered.

Her children carried dishes.

Daughters-in-law helped set the table.

Somebody filled glasses.

Somebody else moved chairs.

One of the grandchildren was told three separate times to stop picking at the macaroni before dinner.

Tim stole something from a pan.

Leola caught him.

“Tim.”

He froze.

“I ain't do nothin'.”

“You chewing.”

Everybody laughed.

Scissors whispered:

“That man understands me.”

Measuring Tape shook herself.

“No. **You** understand that man.”

---

Finally everyone gathered.

Some at the dining table.

Some at another table.

Some wherever another chair could fit.

The younger children squeezed together.

The grown folks shifted around.

Nobody seemed particularly concerned that everything didn't match.

There were too many people for perfection.

And somehow that made it better.

Leola looked around at them.

The room gradually quieted.

Hands reached for hands.

A chain formed around the tables.

Needle watched.

Yarn whispered:

“What they doin'?”

“Giving thanks.”

The basket quieted too.

Leola bowed her head.

Her family followed.

For a moment, the same house that had been full of noise became completely still.

Leola gave thanks.

For the food.

For the home.

For everybody who had made it there.

For those they remembered who weren't sitting at the table anymore.

And then—

her voice caught slightly—

for her granddaughter being safe and able to sit among them after the accident.

Yarn looked toward Needle.

Needle's repaired tip caught the Christmas lights.

Leola's granddaughter lowered her head.

The healing mark near her hairline was still faintly visible.

Leola squeezed her hand.

Yarn moved closer to Needle.

Nobody in the basket said a word.

They understood.

Then Leola finished.

“Amen.”

“Amen,” the family answered.

Scissors whispered:

“Amen.”

And one by one, throughout the basket:

“Amen.”

---

Dinner exploded immediately afterward.

Plates passed.

People talked over one another.

Someone wanted gravy.

Someone else wanted the rolls.

A child announced they didn't like greens.

Three adults simultaneously informed that child they hadn't even tasted them.

Tim started telling a story.

Half the table already knew where it was going and began laughing before he reached the ending.

Scissors listened like he was attending a master class.

Yarn noticed.

“You really do act like him.”

“Who?”

“Tim.”

“I don't know what you mean.”

Tim delivered the punchline.

The whole table erupted.

Scissors immediately repeated the joke to the basket as if he'd invented it.

Measuring Tape stared.

“You just stole that man's joke.”

“I adapted it.”

“You changed two words.”

“Creative interpretation.”

Yarn laughed.

Needle looked from Scissors to Tim.

“I see it.”

Scissors turned.

“See what?”

“Nothing.”

“No, what?”

Needle smiled.

“Nothing at all.”

---

Yarn started noticing the others too.

The way Measuring Tape fussed over everybody while pretending she wasn't fussing.

The way Marker wanted to be included in everything the younger family members were doing.

The way Old Spool could sit quietly through chaos and somehow know exactly when somebody needed a word from him.

These personalities hadn't appeared from nowhere.

They had been listening.

For years.

Living beside Leola.

Hearing her family.

Watching them argue.

Watching them forgive.

Hearing jokes repeated.

Stories retold.

Advice passed down.

The basket family hadn't simply lived **with** Leola.

They had been shaped by the people who filled her life.

Yarn whispered:

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“Scissors got some Tim in him.”

Needle smiled.

“Considerably.”

“And Measuring Tape?”

Needle looked toward the human family.

“Bits and pieces.”

“Marker?”

A little great-grandson ran past carrying a toy.

Marker bounced at the same moment.

Needle and Yarn looked at each other.

“Definitely.”

Yarn smiled.

“We all got pieces of them.”

Old Spool's voice came from behind them.

“That's what family does.”

Yarn turned.

“What?”

“Leaves pieces.”

He looked toward the humans.

“Ways of talking.”

“Ways of laughing.”

“Ways of worrying.”

“Ways of loving.”

Old Spool settled back.

“You stay around love long enough, some of it gets on you.”

Yarn looked at Needle.

He was already looking at her.

---

After dinner came presents.

And Lord, there were presents.

Boxes filled the space around the tree.

Children gathered first.

Adults pretended they weren't just as interested.

Paper tore.

Ribbons flew.

People laughed.

Somebody got slippers.

Someone else got perfume.

There were clothes.

Toys.

Books.

Little things.

Big things.

And then—

Leola brought out the handmade gifts.

The basket family became very still.

They knew these.

They had watched them being born.

The red scarf went to one family member.

Mittens to another.

The cream baby hat went onto the head of one of the youngest great-grandchildren.

Everybody made that sound grown people make when a baby gets something tiny put on its head.

“Awwww.”

Yarn whispered:

“I understand that sound.”

Needle smiled.

Then Leola reached for the final package.

Yarn knew it immediately.

The autumn hat.

Sunset orange.

Deep brown.

The project from the day everything changed.

The granddaughter opened it.

Her face lit up.

“Grandma!”

She pulled the hat out.

“Oh, this is beautiful!”

Yarn nearly burst.

“She said it.”

Needle whispered:

“Yes.”

“She said beautiful.”

“Yes, Yarn.”

“She talkin' about me.”

“Partially.”

Yarn nudged him.

The granddaughter put the hat on.

It sat a little crooked.

Leola laughed and straightened it.

Then the granddaughter hugged her.

Not a quick hug.

A real one.

Leola closed her eyes.

Held her close.

And for a moment, the room around them seemed to disappear.

The accident.

The hospital.

The fear.

The broken Needle.

The waiting.

The repair.

All of it lived somewhere inside that hug.

Yarn's voice softened.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“That's where the hat was goin'.”

“Yes.”

“We didn't know.”

“No.”

Yarn watched Leola touch her granddaughter's face.

“Sometimes you don't know what you're makin' till it gets where it's supposed to be.”

Needle looked at her.

“That sounds like Old Spool.”

“Don't tell him.”

“I heard you,” Old Spool called.

Yarn sighed.

“Of course you did.”

---

Eventually the human celebration began winding down.

But something unexpected happened.

Micky came into Leola's craft room carrying a bag.

She set it beside the old wicker basket.

Yarn looked over.

“What that?”

Needle didn't know.

Micky opened the bag.

Inside was another little collection of crafting tools.

A smaller Needle.

A Crochet Hook.

A few stitch markers.

Some yarn.

A compact Measuring Tape.

And a tiny pair of embroidery Scissors.

Yarn's eyes widened.

“Company.”

The little tools remained still.

Humans were watching.

Micky called toward Leola.

“Momma, I'm leaving my crochet bag here. We still doing that tomorrow?”

Leola answered from the kitchen:

“Long as you don't come in here talkin' about you tired.”

Micky laughed.

“I'll be here.”

“You said that last time.”

“I mean it this time!”

Scissors whispered:

“I like her too.”

Yarn grinned.

Micky placed the bag near Leola's basket.

Then left the room.

Everybody waited.

Voices moved farther away.

The front door opened.

Goodbyes began.

One family left.

Then another.

Hugs.

“Call me when you get home.”

“Text me.”

“Drive careful.”

“Love y'all.”

“Merry Christmas.”

Eventually—

the front door closed.

The house became quiet.

But not dark.

Leola had forgotten—or perhaps chosen not—to switch off the craft-room lamp.

Golden light remained.

Yarn looked toward the visitor bag.

The visitor bag moved.

A small silver Needle slowly peeked over the top.

“Hello?”

The basket erupted.

“HEY!”

“COME ON OUT!”

“WELCOME!”

The little Needle nearly disappeared again.

Scissors shouted:

“DON'T SCARE 'EM!”

“You just screamed!”

“I was welcoming!”

Micky's little embroidery Scissors climbed out.

“Who yelling?”

Our Scissors stood proudly.

“Name's Scissors.”

The smaller pair stared.

“So's mine.”

The basket went silent.

Then Yarn started laughing.

“Oh, this gon' be a long night.”

---

The visiting tools climbed out.

Introductions began.

Again.

Only this time Yarn wasn't the newcomer.

She was part of the welcoming committee.

She understood what that meant.

“I'm Yarn,” she said proudly.

The little Needle nodded.

“I've heard about you.”

Yarn stopped.

“You have?”

“Micky talks about her momma's orange yarn all the time.”

Yarn looked at Needle.

Needle smiled.

Yarn tried not to look too pleased.

Failed completely.

Other yarns emerged.

Red.

Cream.

Green.

Purple.

Brown.

Some were remnants from Leola's recent projects.

Some belonged to Micky.

Some had been tucked away so long even Scissors had forgotten they were there.

Then another Needle appeared.

Smaller.

Younger.

Shinier.

He looked at Needle's repaired tip.

His eyes widened.

“You the one that broke?”

Yarn immediately moved closer to Needle.

The smaller Needle realized he'd been rude.

“I didn't mean—”

Needle smiled.

“It's all right.”

“Did it hurt?”

“Yes.”

“Were you scared?”

Needle looked toward Yarn.

“Yes.”

The younger Needle examined the repair.

“But you still work?”

Needle smiled.

“I had to learn a slightly different way.”

The younger Needle stared at him like he'd just met a legend.

Yarn's heart swelled.

She whispered:

“Handsome.”

Needle glanced at her.

“Don't.”

She grinned.

---

And then—

the craft room had its own Christmas.

No wrapped presents.

No ham.

No macaroni.

No pie.

Scissors remained deeply disappointed about that.

But they had stories.

And scraps.

And memories.

Hook produced a tiny chain she'd been saving.

Marker somehow found a little bell.

Nobody knew where he'd gotten it.

Measuring Tape tied herself into something resembling a bow.

Purple Yarn insisted on telling the story of The Choosing as though he'd personally invented it.

Yarn corrected every exaggeration.

“You did not tell me there'd be a magical kingdom.”

“I implied it.”

“You said there was a castle.”

“Metaphorically.”

“You said the queen wore a crochet cape.”

“Could've happened.”

Scissors loved every word.

The two Scissors started exchanging jokes.

Within ten minutes, Measuring Tape regretted allowing them to meet.

The younger tools listened to Old Spool tell stories.

The yarns compared colors and fibers.

Hook demonstrated a little trick.

Marker tried to organize a game.

Nobody understood the rules.

Including Marker.

And through it all, Needle and Yarn sat beside one another watching their little world grow.

---

Yarn leaned against him.

“Look.”

“I am.”

“Humans got their family.”

“Yes.”

“We got ours.”

Needle looked around.

Scissors was laughing with his smaller counterpart.

Measuring Tape was fussing over the visiting tools.

Marker was attempting to teach a great-grandchild-inspired game that appeared to involve jumping over buttons.

Hook was helping one of Micky's tools untangle a strand.

Old Spool sat in the middle of everything.

Needle smiled.

“Yes.”

“We do.”

Yarn looked toward the doorway.

“Funny.”

“What?”

“Miss Leola's family came in here and filled the whole house.”

Needle nodded.

“And then they left some of themselves behind.”

He looked toward Micky's bag.

Yarn nodded.

“Family keep doing that.”

Needle considered the words.

“Leaving pieces.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“Stories.”

“Ways of talkin'.”

“Ways of laughing.”

“Ways of loving.”

Yarn smiled.

“Old Spool got to you too.”

“He has his moments.”

From across the room:

“I heard that.”

Needle sighed.

Yarn laughed.

---

Much later, the celebration finally slowed.

The visiting tools settled near Leola's basket.

The Christmas-tree lights from the other room painted little patches of red, green and gold across the walls.

Yarn looked toward the unfinished projects.

Then toward Micky's crochet bag.

“She comin' back tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“To work with Miss Leola.”

“Apparently.”

Yarn smiled.

“So tomorrow ain't just another project.”

Needle understood.

“No.”

“Miss Leola gonna teach.”

“Yes.”

Yarn looked around at the younger tools.

“And maybe we are too.”

Needle smiled.

“Perhaps.”

Yarn nudged him.

“That's your word for yes when you don't wanna admit I'm right.”

“It is not.”

“Mmm-hmm.”

---

Before everybody went to sleep, Scissors climbed onto the basket rim.

He cleared his throat.

Nobody paid attention.

He cleared it louder.

Measuring Tape looked up.

“What?”

“I would like to say something.”

The room groaned.

“Oh, Lord.”

“Sit down.”

“Who gave him a speech?”

Scissors opened his blades dramatically.

“I just wanna say…”

He looked around.

At Leola's tools.

Micky's tools.

The old yarn.

The new yarn.

Needle.

Yarn.

“…this was a good Christmas.”

Measuring Tape's expression softened.

“Yes.”

“It was.”

Scissors continued.

“And since I ain't got no presents for none of y'all—”

“You could give us silence,” Hook suggested.

Everybody laughed.

Scissors ignored her.

“—I reckon the best thing I got is this.”

He pointed toward all of them.

“Us.”

The laughter faded.

Scissors suddenly looked embarrassed.

“That's it.”

He climbed down.

Measuring Tape stared.

“That was actually beautiful.”

“Don't tell nobody.”

“You just told everybody.”

“Good night.”

Yarn smiled.

“Tim definitely in that one.”

Needle laughed.

---

The craft room settled.

Yarn rested beside Needle.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“You think families always this messy?”

From three places at once came:

“YES.”

Yarn laughed.

Needle smiled.

“Apparently.”

She looked around.

“Good.”

“Why?”

“Perfect would be boring.”

Needle looked around the room.

At Scissors, who had somehow fallen asleep halfway inside Micky's craft bag.

At Measuring Tape, who was still trying to organize everybody's sleeping arrangements.

At Marker, curled beside the little great-grandchild-inspired toy bell he'd found.

At Purple Yarn, who appeared to be telling a story in his sleep.

At Old Spool, quiet and steady in the middle of all of them.

Then Needle looked toward the other room.

The human family was gone now.

But evidence of them remained everywhere.

A forgotten cup.

A child's ribbon beneath a chair.

Wrapping paper beside the tree.

A plate Leola hadn't carried into the kitchen yet.

The smell of Christmas dinner still hanging in the house.

And Micky's crochet bag.

Needle smiled.

“I suppose you're right.”

Yarn gasped.

“What?”

“You heard me.”

“No, no. Say it again.”

“I will not.”

“Needle just said I was right.”

“I said nothing of the sort.”

“EVERYBODY WAKE UP!”

Needle quickly covered her with a loose scrap.

Yarn laughed underneath it.

“Take this off me!”

“Go to sleep.”

“I can't breathe!”

“You do not breathe.”

“That ain't the point!”

Needle finally removed the scrap.

Yarn emerged laughing.

Then she settled against him again.

For a while they simply watched the Christmas lights.

---

After some time, Yarn spoke more quietly.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“I been thinkin' about that hat.”

“The one Leola gave her granddaughter?”

“Mmm-hmm.”

“What about it?”

Yarn looked toward the doorway.

“That's the thing we was makin' when you broke.”

“Yes.”

“I hated that hat for a little while.”

Needle turned toward her.

“You did?”

“I couldn't look at it without hearin' that sound.”

She didn't have to describe the sound.

Needle knew.

*Snap.*

Yarn continued.

“Every time I saw that brown yarn, every time Miss Leola picked that hat up…”

She swallowed.

“…I went right back there.”

Needle moved closer.

“But tonight…”

She smiled.

“…I saw it on her granddaughter.”

Needle waited.

“And it wasn't the thing that broke you no more.”

Needle's expression softened.

“What was it?”

Yarn looked toward the other room where the granddaughter had been standing earlier.

“A gift.”

A moment passed.

“A good one.”

Needle smiled.

“Yes.”

Yarn touched his repaired tip gently.

“Funny how something can mean one thing…”

“…and then become something else,” Needle finished.

Yarn nodded.

“Maybe that's what makin' is.”

Needle looked at her.

“What?”

“Taking what you got…”

She looked around the room.

“…and turnin' it into what it can be.”

Needle thought about that.

Yarn.

The hat.

His repaired tip.

Leola's frightened hands becoming steady again.

The granddaughter's injury becoming a healing mark.

A ball of yarn becoming a scarf.

A daughter arriving with a craft bag that tomorrow would become another afternoon spent with her mother.

Maybe Yarn was right.

Again.

Needle decided not to tell her.

She'd be unbearable.

---

Yarn looked toward Micky's bag.

“You think that little Needle nervous?”

“Probably.”

“He look at you like you famous.”

Needle sighed.

“I noticed.”

Yarn grinned.

“Needle the Great.”

“Please don't.”

“Sir Needle.”

“Yarn.”

“Master of the Loop.”

“Stop.”

“Survivor of the Great Hospital Journey.”

“I spent most of it inside a case.”

“Legend says he crossed the dangerous halls of Milwaukee—”

“Yarn.”

“—fought three doctors—”

“I met no doctors.”

“—and returned with a silver tip forged in the fires of—”

Needle nudged her.

Yarn burst out laughing.

Across the room, the smaller Needle lifted his head.

“What happened?”

“Nothing,” Needle said.

Yarn whispered:

“Legend business.”

The younger Needle's eyes widened.

Needle groaned.

“Now look what you've done.”

---

A quiet voice came from Micky's bag.

“Mr. Needle?”

The smaller Needle had climbed out again.

Needle looked over.

“Yes?”

“Can I ask you something?”

“Of course.”

The younger Needle approached.

Yarn moved slightly to make room.

“When you broke…”

He hesitated.

“…did you think you wouldn't be useful anymore?”

Needle stopped.

The question changed the room.

Yarn looked at him.

The smaller Needle continued.

“My tip ain't as big as yours. Micky mostly uses me for little things.”

Needle listened.

“Sometimes she picks another needle instead.”

The young Needle looked down.

“I keep thinkin' maybe that means I ain't good enough.”

Needle knew that feeling now.

Before the accident, he might have answered differently.

He might have explained needle sizes.

Purpose.

Technique.

The correct tool for a particular job.

All true.

None of it was what the young Needle needed.

Needle moved closer.

“When I came back, I couldn't work the same way I had before.”

The smaller Needle looked up.

“I had to change my angle.”

“Was that bad?”

“At first, I thought so.”

Yarn listened quietly.

Needle continued.

“I kept trying to move exactly the way I used to.”

“Did it work?”

“No.”

“What'd you do?”

Needle glanced at Yarn.

“She made room.”

Yarn's smile softened.

“And I learned.”

The young Needle thought about that.

Needle continued.

“Being different from another needle doesn't make you less useful.”

He looked toward Micky's bag.

“And somebody choosing another tool for one job doesn't mean they won't need you for the next.”

The younger Needle's posture lifted slightly.

“You think?”

“I know.”

Yarn whispered:

“That's my man.”

Needle tried very hard to remain dignified.

The smaller Needle smiled.

“Thank you.”

He returned to Micky's bag.

Yarn waited until he was gone.

Then—

“My man.”

Needle looked at her.

“You enjoyed that.”

“Very much.”

---

From below came Old Spool's voice.

“Needle.”

“Yes?”

“You did good.”

Needle looked down.

Old Spool's eyes remained closed.

“Thought you were asleep.”

“I was.”

“You spoke.”

“Then apparently I woke up.”

Yarn laughed softly.

Old Spool opened one eye.

“Funny thing about gettin' hurt.”

Nobody interrupted.

“Sometimes after you heal, you find out the thing you went through gave you somethin' somebody else needed.”

Needle looked toward the younger Needle.

Old Spool closed his eye again.

“Don't make the hurt good.”

He paused.

“Just means it didn't get the last word.”

The room became quiet.

Yarn rested her head against Needle.

“I like that.”

“So do I.”

---

A floorboard creaked.

Instantly—

stillness.

Leola appeared in the doorway.

She had gotten back out of bed.

Maybe she'd heard something.

Maybe she'd remembered the light.

Maybe she simply wanted one more look at the room after a long Christmas Day.

She stood there.

Looked at the old wicker basket.

Then at Micky's bag beside it.

A smile crossed her face.

She walked over.

For one frightening moment, everybody thought she would turn off the lamp.

She didn't.

Instead, Leola picked up Micky's bag.

Moved it closer to her own basket.

Right beside it.

Then she adjusted a little blanket near the window.

Picked up the forgotten cup.

And left.

The light stayed on.

The door remained slightly open.

Nobody moved for several seconds after she disappeared.

Then Scissors slowly raised one blade.

“She know.”

Everybody turned.

Measuring Tape whispered:

“Know what?”

Scissors looked toward the two baskets sitting side by side.

“I don't know.”

He lowered his blade.

“But sometimes I think that woman know more than she let on.”

Yarn looked at Needle.

Needle looked toward the doorway.

Neither disagreed.

---

Christmas night became very late.

One by one, everyone finally slept.

Even Scissors.

Even Marker.

Even Purple.

The younger tools curled together inside Micky's bag.

Leola's basket settled beside them.

Two little worlds.

One old.

One newer.

Connected now.

Yarn looked at Needle.

“Tomorrow gonna be different.”

“Yes.”

“Miss Leola and Micky gonna make somethin' together.”

“Yes.”

“What you think?”

Needle smiled.

“I don't guess.”

Yarn stared at him.

After all this time.

“You still sayin' that?”

“I remain consistent.”

Yarn shook herself.

“Well, I'm guessing.”

“Of course you are.”

“I think they gonna make somethin' special.”

Needle looked toward Micky's bag.

“Why?”

“Because Miss Leola ain't just gonna be makin' tomorrow.”

Yarn smiled.

“She gonna be teachin'.”

Needle understood.

Another kind of making.

Not a scarf.

Not a hat.

Not a blanket.

A skill.

Something that couldn't be wrapped in paper.

Something that could pass from one pair of hands into another.

Just like stories.

Just like jokes.

Just like habits.

Just like love.

---

Yarn looked once more toward the human family photographs around Leola's room.

Pictures of children grown.

Grandchildren.

Great-grandchildren.

Birthdays.

Holidays.

People at different ages, in different years, all connected to the same woman whose hands had carried Needle and Yarn through their own first year together.

“Needle?”

“Yes?”

“Miss Leola got a big family.”

“She does.”

Yarn looked around their basket.

“So do we.”

Needle smiled.

“Yes.”

“We do.”

“And it got bigger today.”

Needle looked toward Micky's bag.

“Yes.”

Yarn settled against him.

“That's what family does, ain't it?”

“What?”

“Gets bigger.”

Needle considered.

“Sometimes.”

“Gets messy.”

“Often.”

“Gets loud.”

“Definitely.”

“Gets on your nerves.”

Needle glanced toward Scissors.

“Frequently.”

Yarn laughed.

“And you love 'em anyway.”

Needle looked at her.

“Yes.”

“You do.”

---

The Christmas lights blinked softly in the other room.

Red.

Green.

Gold.

Outside, snow continued falling over Milwaukee.

Inside, leftovers filled Leola's refrigerator.

Wrapping paper waited to be cleaned up in the morning.

Her granddaughter slept safely somewhere beneath the same winter sky, with the autumn hat nearby.

Her children had returned to their own homes.

Freddy.

Micky.

Tim.

Leonard.

Jermaine.

LaRon.

Their voices no longer filled Leola's rooms, but traces of them remained.

A joke.

A dish.

A ribbon.

A memory.

A craft bag.

And in the room where Leola had spent so many years making things with her hands, her old basket sat beside her daughter's.

Inside both were tools shaped not only by the work they had done—

but by the people they had lived beside.

Their laughter.

Their habits.

Their mistakes.

Their patience.

Their love.

Yarn closed her eyes.

Tomorrow, the hands would return.

But this time there would be **four hands** at the table.

Mother and daughter.

Teacher and learner.

Two generations.

And somewhere between them, Needle and Yarn would discover that a pattern could be passed from one person to another without ever becoming smaller.

Just like a family.

Just like love.

**Thread by thread.**

**Stitch by stitch.**

**Generation by generation.**

**Together.**

### — End of Chapter Five —
